# Author's Note

This book uses direct language because the things in it are too hard to say softly.  That is why they are written.

The sequence of events may not be perfectly accurate.  The trip was ten days and it was a blur.  I did not think to record it at the time because I did not yet understand its impact.  I kept a daily blog while it was happening in real time, but that record has been lost.  What remains is memory, photographs, and the weight of what I carried home.  The events are real.  The people are real.  The order may shift because that is how memory works — it organizes by impact, not by calendar.  The things that hit the hardest are the clearest.  The rest I have reconstructed as honestly as I can.

It was our escape of the hard way that prepared us to face it again.

This book is not safe.  If that is difficult to read, it should be.  It was difficult to live.

# CHAPTER.001 — Descent

The conference room had the setup of a negotiation.  Senior Superintendent Kirby John Kraft, Acting Provincial Director of the Laguna Provincial Police Office, sat at the head of the table with his men flanking each side, sergeants or whatever rank they held.  They wore the posture of men accustomed to being in charge of the room.  The chief did all the talking.  His men watched us the way you watch something you haven't decided is a threat or a waste of time.

On our side of the table sat five hope dealers from Salem Heights in black polos and BBSM hats.  Be Bold Street Ministries, The Most Excellent Way.  Matt, Josh, Keith, James, and me.  James had put the whole thing together.  He and Keith were the straight edge guys, accountants who knew how to build a bridge between institutions.  The other three of us were the broken ones.  We had flown across the Pacific with no binder, no program manual, no official presentation.  We had ourselves.  The chief needed something for the surrenderees.  Thousands of people had turned themselves in under Tokhang, Duterte's knock and plead program, and now they were a logistics problem.  You cannot shoot all of them.  You cannot feed all of them.  You cannot put them back on the streets because Manila is watching the numbers.  The surrenderees needed to go to a program but the programs were unstructured.  The chief needed a solution and he had run out of ideas that didn't involve brutality.

He explained the problem like a man explaining why a machine is broken.  The surrenderees were addicts.  Addicts cannot be fixed.  The holding cells were overcrowded.  The farm was the only option he had been given and the farm was not something he wanted to talk about in detail.  Nobody could fix addicts, he told us.  That was the wall.  That was the end of every conversation he had with Manila, with the mayor, with his own men.  Nobody could fix addicts.

Three addicts, fixed, and two men who had never touched the stuff but showed up anyway, stared blankly back at him.

I don't think any of us said it in that moment.  We didn't need to announce it like a reveal on a television show.  It wasn't a strategy.  It was just the truth sitting on our side of the table.  Three of us were living proof that the most excellent way worked.  We had been the ones on the list.  We had been the ones people gave up on.  Keith and James had never touched the stuff, but they showed up anyway because the most excellent way is not only for the broken — it is also for the ones who stand beside them.  The difference between us and the men in his holding cells was that somebody had not quit on us.

The chief was not what I expected.  I expected a man who enjoyed the hard way.  I expected the face of the machine.  What I got was a man carrying a weight he did not ask for.  Manila told him to be strong and strong meant bodies.  His officers were executing operations that broke them in ways they would not talk about for years.  He sat at the head of that table because he had all the power in the room, but power is not the same thing as control.  He controlled nothing.  He was being driven by a mandate from the president that treated human lives like arithmetic.  Reduce the number.  Any method is acceptable.  Report the results.

We presented ourselves.  That was all we had.

Matt opened.  He looked at the chief of police in a country where being a drug addict could get you killed and said, "I heard you were killing drug addicts, so I thought you ought to know that I'm the worst one."

The room did not know what to do with that.  I don't think the chief had ever had someone walk into his station and volunteer for the list.  Josh and I were quick to add ourselves to make sure they didn't take Matt away.  If one of us was on the list, all of us were on the list.  That was the deal.  Three Americans surrendering themselves to the hard way in a conference room in Calamba.  We were not there to observe from a safe distance.  We were the people the hard way was designed for, sitting across from it, daring it to take us.  We told the chief of police in Calamba that we used to hurt people for fun.  That our depravity was so deep we did not recognize it as depravity while we were in it.  We told him about dealing.  Not the sanitized version, not the version you tell a church on a Sunday morning.  We told him about the power.  What it feels like to have a neighborhood depend on you, to have people afraid of you, to control who gets well and who gets sick.  We told him about the money and the respect and the way everyone moves when you walk into a room.

Then we told him about losing it.  The crushing lows of homelessness.  Sleeping in places a person should not have to sleep.  Watching your body fall apart.  Watching the people who feared you step over you.  The distance between the top of the drug trade and the bottom of the street is shorter than anyone on either end believes.

We told him how many people we had met since then and helped.  Not with programs.  Not with frameworks.  With our own hands and our own stories.  People we sat with while they detoxed.  People we drove to meetings.  People we picked up off the same streets we had been picked up from.  We had no binder to hand him.  We just came with brutal honesty.  The pitch was five people who had been exactly where his surrenderees were, sitting upright and breathing and looking him in the eye.

We told him about The Most Excellent Way, about what it looked like to walk alongside someone through recovery instead of dragging them to a cell.  We told him surrenderees could help other surrenderees.  That the people closest to the problem were the ones most capable of addressing it.  That the answer to addiction was not a bullet or a cage but a community of people who had been there and come out the other side.

At some point we told him we were trying to find the support they needed.  He looked at his men, looked back at us, and said, "We will take whatever you have."

The room laughed.  It was the first honest sound anyone had made since we sat down.  His sergeants laughed.  We laughed.  It was the laugh of a man who has been holding his breath for months and someone finally told him he could exhale.  Whatever you have.  Not a negotiation.  Not a counter-proposal.  A man at the end of his options saying yes before he even knew what he was saying yes to.

That laugh changed the room.  The negotiation setup collapsed.  The us and them dissolved into something that was not quite trust but was close enough to work with.  He wasn't evaluating our proposal anymore.  He was deciding whether we were real.

I think that's why he took us downstairs.

It was not on the schedule.  Nobody had planned for the chief of police to walk five Americans into the basement of his station.  But something in the way we talked about the surrenderees, the way we talked about them as people who could recover instead of problems to be processed, something in that cracked a door he did not have to open.

The staircase went down one flight.  The chief walked ahead of us like a man showing you a wound he's been hiding.

On the way down, Kraft ducked his head into a side room.  He said something I could not hear, something short, the tone of a man issuing a correction he wished he did not have to make.  The crying stopped.

I had not realized there was crying until it stopped.  The absence was louder than the sound had been.  Whatever was happening in that room, it had been happening long enough that my ears had incorporated it into the background noise of the descent.  The chief told them to stop and they stopped and the silence that replaced it pressed against every surface of that staircase.

Then the smell hit.  The air changed before the light did.  It got heavier, wetter, sour with the smell of too many bodies in not enough space.

The cells were on both sides of us.  In front and behind.  We stood in the middle of it, not looking in from the outside but surrounded.  Bars to the left, bars to the right, men pressed against them in every direction.  Six bunkbeds.  I counted them because counting was something to do with my eyes that was not looking at the men.  Six bunkbeds and at least sixty men.  They were on the beds, under the beds, along the walls, sitting with their knees drawn up because there was not enough floor to stretch out.  The math of it doesn't make sense until you see it.  You think you understand overcrowding and then you stand in a room where ten men share a space designed for one and you realize the word overcrowding was invented by people who had never been in this room.

I remember the fans.  Lots of fans.  They were everywhere in that room, spinning and pushing air that had no business being called air across the bodies of sixty men.  The fans kept everyone evaporating their sweat and that is what you breathed.  The moisture of sixty men being circulated by cheap electric fans bolted to whatever surface would hold them.  The heat did not go away.  The fans just moved it around and gave it texture.

Kraft did not need to let us in there.  He did not need to show us the worst thing happening under his authority.  He could have kept us in the conference room, shaken our hands, and sent us back to our hotel.  Instead he took us down the staircase and showed us the truth.  That was courage.  A man inside a brutal system chose to show strangers the thing he could not show anyone else because nobody else had walked into his station and talked about addicts like they were people.  He was not showing us the system's success.  He was showing us his burden.  He was asking for help the only way a man in his position could.

Matt prayed.  He had the mic.  Our first stop on the ministry tour and it was a cage and Matt did not skip a beat.  He opened his mouth and prayed over sixty men in six bunkbeds the way he did everything else on this trip — without hesitation, without performance, without the pause that would have told the room he was afraid.  Maybe he was afraid.  He did not show it.

I did not pray.  I stood there.  I have been in jail enough times to know how to put on a good face and I put one on.  But standing outside that cage was less comfortable than being inside one ever was in Oregon.  Inside a cage you know the rules.  Outside a cage watching sixty men reach through bars while your friend prays over them is a different kind of horror.  I was reliving the scene in my head while it was still happening.  My body was in the room.  My mind was trying to leave.

As long as Matt was praying, we were present.  As long as we were present, whatever was happening in that side room was paused.  His prayer was the length of the reprieve.  I knew it while he was praying.  I knew that when he said amen and we walked back up those stairs, the silence would end.

That prayer could not be long enough.  And it was not mine.  It was Matt's.

While he prayed I could not stop thinking about how many of them we were the last choice they got.  That the farm was their next stop.  That tomorrow another set of faces would be staring back from behind those bars and the men reaching through them right now would be gone.  This was not a prison where you serve a sentence and come out.  This was a holding cell.  The word holding does not mean kept.  It means waiting.  They were waiting to be moved and where they were moving was not somewhere you come back from.  We were the last voice some of them would hear that said they were worth saving.  And I was doing math.  Counting bunkbeds.  Counting men.  Counting how many would be gone tomorrow and how many new faces would replace them.  Compartmentalizing the horror the same way bureaucracy does — turning people into numbers because numbers are survivable and faces are not.  That is what the mind does when it cannot process what the eyes are seeing.  Manila ran Tokhang on math.  Reduce the number.  Report the results.  I was running on the same math standing in its basement.  Not because I was the system.  Because math was the only thing between me and the floor.

Few people were prepared to see what was below those stairs.  I do not think I was one of them.  Above us, six-wheeled vehicles drove up and down the streets of Calamba.  The officers on those vehicles wore masks.  The men carrying out the hard way covered their faces.  The state gave them the authority and the masks gave them the permission.  You cannot do what they were doing with your face showing because the hard way knows what it is.  It hides.  Behind masks, behind language, behind euphemisms like the farm and words like tokhang.  The machinery of the hard way doing its work.  Below us, the product of that machinery sat in cages.  Men who had surrendered because the alternative was a body on the pavement with a cardboard sign.  They had raised their hands to the state and the state had put them here.

We made the call.  The call to Jesus.  For many of them it was their last chance to surrender to anything that was not going to destroy them.  They had surrendered to Duterte's program and it gave them six bunkbeds and sixty bodies and a room where the crying doesn't stop unless visitors come downstairs.  This was a different surrender.  This was not hands up because a six-wheeled vehicle is outside your house.  This was hands up because someone is offering you something other than the hard way.

I did not see one person not reaching through those bars with their hands up when the call happened.

Not one.

The hands came from in front of us and behind us.  From both sides.  Three rows deep, men pressed against each other to get their hands through the bars.  The ones in the front were crushed against the iron by the weight of the men behind them.  The second row reached over shoulders and between heads.  The third row, the ones farthest from us, the ones who could not even see us, pressed their bodies into the men in front of them to push their arms through.  Three rows of human beings compressing themselves against each other and against the bars in every direction because all of them wanted to reach toward something.  The ones on the beds, the ones who could not walk because of what had been done to them, they reached too.  They could not stand.  They could not press themselves into the rows at the bars.  They lay where the torture had left them and stretched their arms out from the beds toward the sound of the call.  Sixty men in a basement with the fans spinning and the sweat evaporating off their skin and every single one of them stretching through iron.  Hands up.  All of them.  Men who had nothing left, who had been told by their own government that they were not worth fixing, three rows deep pressing against each other to reach four strangers who had just told them they used to be exactly where they are.

I have been in rooms where the call goes out and half the people shift in their seats.  I have been in churches where a handful respond.  I have been on streets where one person out of twenty will stop and listen.  I have never been in a room where every single person reached.  The bars did not stop them.  They pushed their arms through and raised their hands and in that moment the holding cell in the basement of a police station in Calamba became the most honest church I have ever stood in.

That is what I remember.  Not exactly what I said to God.  I remember the hands.  I remember the heat and the smell and the sound of sixty men responding to the only offer anyone had made them that did not involve a cell or a farm or a bullet.  I remember that Kraft watched it happen and did not say a word.  I think he was seeing for the first time what he had been hoping was possible.

The chief walked us back up.  Nobody spoke.  There is a particular silence that follows when a group of people has seen something together that none of them were prepared for.  It is not the silence of having nothing to say.  It is the silence of having too much to say and no words that fit.

We walked out of the station into the sun.  The Philippines does not care what you just saw.  The heat and the light hit you the same way they did before you went down those stairs.  The jeepneys are still loud.  The street vendors are still selling.  The world continued while sixty men sat in six bunkbeds and someone in a side room was told to stop.

Five hope dealers stood in the parking lot of a police station in Calamba and did not say anything to each other for what felt like a long time.  Five addicts, fixed, who had just seen what happens to the ones nobody fixes.

That is why we went to Southblock 7.  That is why we went door to door in Calauan telling anyone who would listen that nobody is broken beyond fix.  That is why we led the recovery programs that gave the surrenderees somewhere to go that was not the cells and not the farm.  Because we had been in the room.  We had seen the alternative.  The hard way has a smell and a sound and a silence, and once you have experienced it you either go home and forget or you go to the streets and fight it with the only thing that has ever worked.

We did not speak one harsh word in their country.  We did not raise a hand.  We did not condemn the chief or his men or the system that built those holding cells.  We treated everyone with respect and love because that is the most excellent way and because five of us were living proof that it works.

Nobody can fix addicts.

We were sitting right there.

The smell of that basement followed us out into the sun.  It followed us to the streets.  It is following me still.


# CHAPTER.002 — Knock

The word sounds gentle if you do not know what it means.  Tokhang.  Bisayan.  *Toktok* — knock.  *Hangyo* — plead.  Knock and plead.  It sounds like something a neighbor does when they need to borrow rice.  It sounds like mercy.  That was the point.  You name a thing gently when you need people to accept it before they understand what it is.

Rodrigo Duterte was inaugurated on June 30, 2016.  Tokhang launched the next day.  Not a week later.  Not after a transition period or a policy review.  The next day.  The speed tells you it was never a policy.  It was a promise being collected.  Duterte had campaigned on killing drug users and drug dealers and the people who voted for him did so knowing exactly what they were voting for.  He told them during the campaign that he would fill Manila Bay with bodies.  They elected him by six million votes.

The program worked like this.  Barangay officials — neighborhood captains, essentially — compiled watch lists.  Names of suspected drug users and dealers in their jurisdiction.  The lists were supposed to come from intelligence.  They came from rumors.  They came from grudges.  They came from neighbors who owed money or wanted a property or had an argument about a rooster.  They came from barangay captains who were paid to add a name or remove one.  The lists were as accurate as the people who wrote them, and the people who wrote them were human beings with debts and jealousies and scores to settle.  Your name on a list was not evidence.  It was a sentence.  I remember the men in the holding cells below the Calamba station and I think about those lists now.  Every one of those sixty men started as a name on a piece of paper compiled by someone who may or may not have known them.

If your name was on the list, you had two options.  Surrender or wait.

The ones who waited got the knock.

Between ten at night and four in the morning, convoys entered the neighborhoods.  Six-wheeled armored vehicles, V-150 transports, rolling through streets built for tricycles and pedestrians.  The officers on those vehicles wore masks.  Balaclavas.  Ski masks.  The kind of covering that exists for one reason, which is to allow the person beneath it to do something they cannot do with their face showing.  They cordoned streets.  They kicked in doors.  They executed operations that the reports would later describe with a single word: *nanlaban*.  He resisted.

Nanlaban.  It appeared in police report after police report like a stamp.  He resisted, so we killed him.  She resisted, so we killed her.  The seventeen-year-old resisted, so we killed him.  The consistency was its own confession.  Every suspect resisted.  Every encounter required lethal force.  Every body found with a drop gun beside it, a .38 revolver placed in the hand of someone who had been breathing ten minutes earlier.  Drugs materialized beside the dead.  Sachets of shabu appeared on kitchen tables in houses that had been torn apart.  The evidence arrived after the killing because the killing was the point and the evidence was the paperwork.

Duterte said it out loud.  That was the thing about him.  He did not bother with the pretense.  "If you know of any addicts, go ahead and kill them yourself," he said at his inauguration.  Not in a closed meeting.  Not in a leaked memo.  At his inauguration, in public, to the nation.  "I'd be happy to slaughter them," he said, comparing himself to Hitler and getting the Holocaust body count wrong in the same sentence.  "I will protect you," he told police.  "If you kill a thousand persons, I will protect you."  He told officers to plant evidence on suspects, then when journalists reported it he called it a joke.  Everything was a joke.  The killing was serious and the language around it was a joke and the country had to decide which one to believe, and sixty-five million voters had already made that decision.

The bodies appeared in the streets and in the alleys and along the rivers.  They appeared with cardboard signs placed on their chests or tied around their necks.  *Pusher Ako, Huwag Tularan.*  I am a pusher.  Do not emulate.  The signs were written in marker on torn cardboard, the handwriting of someone who wanted you to read it quickly and understand it completely.  The bodies were a message and the signs were the translation.  This is what happens.  This will happen to you.  The cardboard was the cheapest material available for a public service announcement and it was brutally effective.

The official count from the Philippine National Police acknowledged six thousand two hundred to six thousand six hundred killed in operations.  Human Rights Watch and Amnesty International estimated between twelve thousand and thirty thousand dead when you included the vigilante killings that the police did not claim and did not investigate.  The gap between those numbers is a field of bodies that nobody in Manila wanted to count.  Twelve thousand is the floor.  Thirty thousand is the ceiling.  Somewhere between those numbers is the truth, and the truth was lying in the streets with cardboard on its chest.

In August 2017, officers killed a seventeen-year-old boy named Kian delos Santos.  The police report said he was a drug courier.  The police report said nanlaban.  He resisted.  But Kian was killed in an alley with CCTV cameras, and the cameras showed something different from the report.  They showed officers dragging a teenager through the dark.  They showed him on his knees.  They showed what nanlaban actually looked like when someone was watching.  The footage went public.  The country saw it.  Three officers were eventually convicted in November 2018, the same month I was in Calamba looking at the product of the system that killed him.  Three officers held accountable out of a machine that had produced twelve thousand bodies.  Three convictions in two and a half years.  The math of accountability was worse than the math of the holding cells.

But the dead were not the largest number.  The largest number was the living.

By 2018, between 1.2 and 1.8 million people had surrendered.  They walked into police stations and barangay halls and signed their names on forms and raised their hands and said yes, I am on the list, I am what you say I am, I surrender.  They did this because the alternative was a knock at two in the morning and a cardboard sign.  They surrendered because surrender sounded like it came with a program.  The government promised rehabilitation.  Counseling.  Livelihood support.  Medical assistance.  The Community-Based Drug Rehabilitation Program.  CBDRP.  It had an acronym, which meant it was real.

What the surrenderees actually got was Zumba.

I need you to sit with that.  A million people turned themselves in to the state, put their names on a government list, and the rehabilitation program they were promised was group Zumba sessions and community prayer.  That was the program.  That was the infrastructure the Philippine government built to address the addiction crisis it had declared a national emergency.  You surrendered.  You signed the form.  Your name went on a list that made you more visible to the same officers driving the six-wheeled vehicles.  And then someone taught you an aerobics routine and told you to pray about it.

The list was the cruelest part.  Before you surrendered, your name might have been on a watch list compiled by a barangay captain with a grudge.  After you surrendered, your name was on a government list compiled by the state itself.  You had confirmed what the watch list only alleged.  You had given the machinery your address, your face, your confession.  If the Zumba did not fix you — and I don't need to tell you whether Zumba fixes methamphetamine addiction — you were now a documented failure of a government program, which made you a justified target for the next operation.  Surrender made you safer for a week and more vulnerable forever.

The prisons could not hold them.  The Philippines already had among the most overcrowded prison systems on the planet before Duterte took office.  Manila City Jail operated at five hundred percent capacity.  Five times the number of human beings the building was designed to contain.  Sleeping in shifts.  Families paying guards to bring food and bedding.  The cells I saw in Calamba were not an aberration.  They were the national standard.  Six bunkbeds and sixty men was the system working as designed, because the system was never designed to hold people.  It was designed to process them.  In through the door, name on the list, body in a cell, out to the farm.

The farm.  The chief in Calamba said the word without saying what it meant.  He said it the way you say the name of a place you have sent people to that you do not want to describe.  The farm was what came after the holding cells.  It was where the surrenderees went when the cells were full and the Zumba had not fixed them and Manila needed the numbers to move.  Nobody used the word farm to mean a farm.  It was a word that covered whatever happened next, and whatever happened next was not something the chief wanted to talk about in detail.  The farm was the end of the sentence that started with a knock on a door at two in the morning.  Tokhang to nanlaban to the holding cell to the farm.  Knock, plead, resist, disappear.

I arrived in Calamba on November 1, 2018.  Two and a half years into this.  By then the drug war was not a crisis.  It was the weather.  It was the thing that happened and continued happening and people lived around it the way you live around monsoon season.  The six-wheeled vehicles drove the streets and the masks were on the officers and the cardboard signs had stopped being shocking and started being scenery.  Twelve thousand dead or twenty thousand dead or thirty thousand dead and the country had absorbed it.  The polls showed majority support.  The president's approval ratings stayed above seventy percent.  A democracy had voted for mass killing and then approved of the result and the word for that is normalization and normalization is the thing that should terrify you more than the killing itself, because killing can be stopped but a country that has learned to accept killing has to unlearn it, and unlearning is slower and harder and nobody has written the program for that either.

That is what we walked into.  Five Americans from Salem Heights with no binder and no program manual.  Into a system that had produced a million surrenderees and given them Zumba.  Into a police station where the chief had been told to be strong and strong meant bodies.  Into holding cells where the men could not walk because of what had been done to them.  Into a country that had normalized the knock the way you normalize a commute.

The chief sat at the head of that table because he had the authority to kill and the authority meant nothing because it did not solve the problem.  He could fill the cells and empty them to the farm and fill them again and the drugs would still be there and the addicts would still be there and the neighborhoods would still be broken.  The hard way gave him a cycle, not a solution.  And the only alternative Manila had given him was a dance class.

When five Americans who used to be exactly where his surrenderees were walked into his conference room and told him the truth about themselves, he did not evaluate our credentials.  He did not ask for our methodology.  He said we'll take whatever you have, and he laughed, because for two and a half years the only thing anyone had brought him was a body count and a Zumba instructor.

We brought whatever we had.  It was not much.  It was ourselves.

He took us downstairs.  You already know what we found.

Tokhang.  Knock and plead.  The gentlest word for the hardest thing.  A whole country knocked on, a whole country pleaded with, and the only answer anyone had given was more knocking.  Harder.  Louder.  With masks on and guns drawn between ten at night and four in the morning.

We knocked too.  We knocked on the chief's door and on the doors in Southblock 7 and on the bars of the holding cells.  But our knock was the original meaning of the word.  The one before Duterte took it and made it into something that required a mask.  We knocked the way a neighbor knocks.  We knocked the way you knock when you need to borrow something, except what we were offering to lend was the proof that the men on the other side of the door were not beyond repair.

The word still means what it means.  *Toktok.*  Knock.  Somebody has to reclaim it.

And somebody had to answer the door.  In Calauan, they did.


# CHAPTER.003 — Roofless

The church in Calauan was a house.  It did not have glass windows.  The air came in and the sound went out and there was no barrier between the worship and the street.  A guitar and two singers led from the front.  The worship slides were written on a large post-it note stuck to the wall because that is what they had and that is what they used.  The worship was in English.  The service was in both English and Tagalog, switching between them the way bilingual families switch at the dinner table, mid-sentence sometimes, without announcement.

Someone recited Romans from memory.  I cannot recall which chapter.  I remember the sound of it more than the words, the rhythm of scripture spoken by a person who had carried it in their head long enough that it came out like breathing.  There was no podium.  There was no sound system.  There was a person standing in a house with no glass windows reciting the word of God from the only place it could not be confiscated.

The church was packed.  There were no seats anywhere.  People stood along the walls and filled the doorway and the street outside was full of people who could not get in.  They listened from the street through windows that had no glass.  The church was roofless in every sense that mattered.  No barriers.  No velvet ropes.  No ushers telling you the sanctuary is full.  If you could hear it you were part of it.  The building could not contain what was happening inside it and so it spilled out into Southblock 7 and anyone walking by became the congregation.

We ate together afterwards.  Adobo chicken with fork and spoon.  The meal happened the way meals happen when a community feeds itself, without formality, styrofoam togo containers appearing and being filled and people eating standing or sitting wherever there was space.  The food was the bridge between the service and the streets.  You worship together, you eat together, and then you go to work.

The feeding took preparation.  I had this brilliant idea that we could buy food from a restaurant near the hotel because they had listed togo containers for about a dollar.  That turned out to be the price for the container.  The food was extra.  So instead we went to their warehouse store and filled shopping carts.  Americans buying in bulk in a country where people buy one package of ramen at a time.  The staff had to go to the back repeatedly because they did not usually keep that much of anything stocked.  We cleaned them out of things they had never sold that many of at once.  I remember the looks on the workers' faces, the math they were doing trying to figure out what five Americans in Hope Dealer shirts needed with this much food.

It was already a Christmas celebration at the mall.  The tree was up in early November.  They served rice alongside the mashed potatoes at KFC.  The Philippines takes the things America exports and makes them its own.  Christmas in November.  Rice with your mashed potatoes.  Fast food that acknowledges what people actually eat.  I liked it.  It was honest in a way American KFC is not.

After eating we hit the streets.  Door to door through Southblock 7.  Concrete block houses with no roofs.  I have been in neighborhoods in the States that people call rough and I do not use that word lightly but Southblock 7 had a texture to it that I had not encountered.  The houses were structures.  Walls and floors and sometimes a roof and sometimes not.  Building materials along the path told you that people were trying.  The construction was perpetual and never finished.  You build what you can with what you have and when you run out of materials you live in what you built so far.

The shops in Southblock 7 were houses.  People had a roll-up cage on the front of their home and that was the store.  You could buy a package of top ramen or a can of soup or a piece of fruit through the cage.  There was no real store.  No market with aisles and a register.  Just houses that were also businesses, the cage rolling up in the morning and rolling down at night, commerce and family happening in the same structure separated by iron mesh.  People did not go grocery shopping.  They came home from a hard day of work with hopefully a package of ramen to share with five people for dinner.  That was the economy.  One package.  Five mouths.  The store existed at the scale of the poverty, selling single servings because nobody could afford a pantry.

There was an internet cafe.  People were addicted to gaming.  Stealing to get by just to play video games.  I say that not to mock it but because addiction does not care about your income level or your proximity to substances.  Addiction finds whatever is available.  In a neighborhood where a package of ramen is dinner for a family, the internet cafe offered the only escape that cost less than drugs and the kids who could not afford either one stole to sit in front of a screen.  The hard way had no answer for this.  You cannot shoot a gaming addiction.  You cannot send a teenager to the farm for playing too much.  But the desperation underneath it was the same desperation that drove every other addiction in Southblock 7.  A life with no hope will find something to disappear into.

I heard arguments coming from the houses.  A father came home drunk and did not bring dinner.  Five people waiting on one package of ramen and the man who was supposed to carry it home drank it instead.  That is what the drug war sounded like at the household level.  Not cartels and kilos and international trafficking.  A family screaming at a man who chose the bottle over a package of noodles.  The distance between that argument and the holding cells was shorter than anyone in Manila wanted to admit.  That father was one knock away from the six-wheeled vehicle.  One neighbor's report.  One bad night.  One voice too loud.  And the family that was angry at him for not bringing dinner would be the same family destroyed when the police took him away.  Everything in Calauan served double duty because there was not enough of anything to dedicate a building to one purpose.  A house is a house and a store and sometimes a church.

We knocked on doors and talked to people about Jesus and about hope.  Not the sanitized version.  The same brutal honesty we brought to the chief's conference room and to the stage at Bucal High School.  Nobody is broken beyond fix.  We said it at every door.  Some doors opened.  Some did not.

We did not talk to people outside so much as everyone was already outside.  Americans in Calauan.  That does not happen.  Everyone was looking.  But the one person who struck me more than anyone else was not gawking.  She invited us into her house.

We said our peace.  We told her about Jesus and about hope and about nobody being broken beyond fix.  She listened.  Then she went to another room and came back with a bible.  She said she was sorry it had collected dust.  But seeing us here gave her hope.

She made cards for a living.  Like Hallmark.  A woman in Southblock 7 writing sentiments about love and hope and occasion for other people to give each other, and her own bible was collecting dust in another room.  She knew the words.  She made her living arranging them.  But somewhere between being a worship leader and making greeting cards in a neighborhood where dinner is one package of ramen, the bible stopped being something she opened.

I do not know what happened in between.  I do not know what the drug war took from her or what she gave up or what broke.  I know she had not thrown the bible away.  She kept it through whatever had happened and when five Americans walked through Southblock 7 and one of them ended up in her house saying the words she used to say, she went and found it and apologized for the dust.

Being an encouragement in a discouraging place made that day worthwhile.  I say that knowing how small it sounds.  One woman with a dusty bible on a street where the six-wheeled vehicles drive and the holding cells fill and the farm waits at the end of every failed chance.  But the most excellent way does not operate at scale.  It operates at the door.  One knock, one conversation, one woman who makes cards about hope for a living remembering that hope was supposed to be for her too.  That is the pattern.  Not enforcement.  Encouragement.

After the doors I went to the hill.

The path led up from Southblock 7 into what became jungle quickly.  Building materials lined the path the same way they lined the streets below, marking where people had tried to build and how far they had gotten.  The path kept going.  There were people up there.  People who needed to hear about Jesus the same as anyone at the doors below.  I started climbing.

Leonardo Fabella, the assistant pastor, told me to not go any further.

I looked at the path continuing up into the green and I looked at the assistant pastor and I understood that he was not making a suggestion.  It was not about the terrain.  It was not about the heat.  It was about what was up there and what would happen to an American walking alone into it.  I would likely not have come back from that walk without a ransom.  There were people on that hill who would see an American and see a payday, and no amount of brutal honesty about chasing girls and hurting people was going to change that calculation in the minutes between meeting them and being taken.

So I stopped.  I stood on the path with the building materials and the jungle and I looked up the hill at the place where the most excellent way met its limit.  Not its failure.  Its limit.  There are places love wants to go that are not yours to enter.  There are people who need to hear about Jesus who you are not the person to reach.  The assistant pastor knew that.  He lived here.  He knew the hill the way I knew the streets back home and when he told me to stop I trusted him because that is what the most excellent way looks like when it is being practiced by someone who has been doing it longer than you in a place more dangerous than you understand.

I came back down.  The church with no glass windows was still there.  The street was quieter now.  The woman with the bible was inside her house.  Southblock 7 continued being Southblock 7.  Nothing had changed except that a few more people had been told they were not broken beyond fix and one woman had found her bible and an American had learned that the hill has a limit and the limit is not cowardice, it is wisdom.

Assistant Pastor Leonardo Fabella stayed.  Pastor Anthony Mariano, the chairman, stayed.  Revealing Christ's Glory Global Ministries stayed.  When I flew home they were still knocking on doors in Southblock 7 and they are probably knocking on them right now.  The most excellent way is not a mission trip.  A mission trip is a spark.  The most excellent way is the people who keep the fire going after the spark leaves.

I think about that hill.  I think about who lives up there and whether anyone has reached them.  I think about the building materials on the path and the people trying to build something with what they have.  I think about the assistant pastor standing on the path telling me to stop and how that was the most loving thing anyone said to me in the Philippines.

The church had no glass windows.  The houses had no roofs.  The path had no end I was allowed to see.  Everything in Calauan was open and unfinished and waiting for someone to keep building.

But the stage was waiting.  A school full of kids who needed to hear from someone who had been where they were headed.


# CHAPTER.004 — The Stage

The six-wheeled vehicles were visible from the stage.  They moved along the highway beyond the school grounds, unhurried, routine, the way a predator patrols a territory it has already claimed.  On the other side of the highway a mountain rose up and the stage had a thatch roof over it that framed the view like a window into the country you were actually standing in.  The students would give their attention to you or to the vehicles.  Both were telling them something about their future.

We went in and met the principal first.  The conversation was not about what we would say to the students.  It was about assuring him this was not a one-day thing.  Everyone who came through offered a one-day thing.  An assembly, a presentation, a photo opportunity, and then the airplane home.  The chief needed someone who wasn't going to quit on them and so did the principal.  Different building, same desperation, same question behind the eyes: are you real or are you tourism?

I could see students milling outside between classes while we talked.  They moved the way students move everywhere in the world, loose and loud and unaware that adults in an office nearby were discussing their futures.  They wore uniforms.  They carried bags.  They looked like students in Salem Heights except for the six-wheeled vehicles on the highway behind them.

Salem Heights was not always what it became.  The church had its own hard way before it found the most excellent way.  When addicts first started showing up, the congregation did not meet them with grace.  The culture had to change.  The very people who would become hope dealers were not welcomed the way new ones are welcomed now.  The early ones — people like me, like Matt, like Josh — we were the disruption.  We were the surrenderees of Salem Heights, showing up broken in a place that did not yet know what to do with broken people.  The church changed because we stayed.  And because Pastor Matt MacCollin would not give up on us.  He ran the Most Excellent Way at Salem Heights and he was the first knucklehead of the group.  Keep showing up, he would say.  That was the whole method.  Not a curriculum.  Not a strategy.  Keep showing up.  He showed up for us when nobody else did and we showed up for each other and eventually the culture bent around the honesty until it broke open into something new.  Not because we found a better church.  Because we stayed in the one God put us in.

What I did not know then and what I am only now putting together is that MacCollin was not just our pastor.  He is the International Director of The Most Excellent Way.  The same program he ran for us at Salem Heights, he brought to the Philippine National Police.  He worked directly with Kraft in Davao, implementing TMEW as a values formation tool for officers — not just for surrenderees in the community but for the police themselves.  Weekly squad interactive meetings.  Life coaching sessions.  The beatitudes taught to men in uniform.  The same ten attitudes of victorious living that he taught us in Oregon, he taught to officers in the Philippines.

The bridge between a church in Salem Heights and a one-star general at PNP national headquarters is one man who kept showing up.  MacCollin said it to us and he said it to Kraft and he said it to officers in Davao.  The knucklehead who would not give up on addicts in Oregon is the same knucklehead who would not give up on the Philippine National Police.  Keep showing up.  That is the engine.  That is what we were carrying across the Pacific.  Not just a message.  A ministry that was already inside the system we were walking into.  The same thing that happened at Salem Heights had already begun happening inside the police force.  But MacCollin was not in the conference room.  He handed us the digitized cards.  He built the engine.  We were the ones who carried it across the Pacific and sat across from Kraft and volunteered for the list and descended the staircase and went door to door in Southblock 7.  MacCollin built the most excellent way into a global ministry.  We were the five knuckleheads who walked it into a room where a man with a province full of holding cells said he would take whatever we had.

We had brought at least two suitcases full of Hope Dealer shirts.  Back home, shirt sales were how we funded the ministry.  People in Salem Heights supported Be Bold by literally buying a shirt.  That was the business model.  We knew we had packed too many, but we also knew what we were going to do with them.  We gave them away.  To everyone we met.  The chief of police is holding one in the photo we took in his conference room.  The RCGGM partners wore them.  The translators wore them.  The kids on the street wore them.  Sometimes we gave them the shirts off our backs because we had already emptied the suitcases and someone was standing in front of us who needed to hear they were a hope dealer too.  By the end of the trip the shirts had spread through Calamba and Calauan the same way the program did, person by person, one at a time, until people we had never met were walking the streets wearing the words Hope Dealer on their chest.  The funding mechanism became the gift.  The thing that paid for the trip became the thing we left behind.

The program we were starting was called Morals.  It was all scripture.  But the delivery was not what anyone in that school had seen before.  We were not there to lecture students about right and wrong.  We were there to teach the teachers how to reach students.  The students were going to hear us that day, but the teachers were the real audience.  We were creating a pattern of honesty rather than enforcement.  That was the whole thing.  Every school these kids attended ran on enforcement.  Every interaction with authority in their lives ran on enforcement.  The government enforced the drug war.  The police enforced Tokhang.  The teachers enforced discipline.  We were showing them a different tool.  Stand in front of a room and tell the truth about yourself and watch what happens.

The assembly gathered.  Matt went first.  Then Josh.  They told the same stories we had told the chief in the conference room.  Hurting people.  Drug addiction.  The power and the collapse and the recovery.  They told students the same things we told adults because the truth does not have an age-appropriate version.  A teenager in Calamba whose neighbor was dragged out of his house last month does not need a sanitized curriculum.  He needs a person who has been where he is standing in front of him saying I was there and I am here now and the distance between those two things is not impossible.

Then I was up.

I remember the thatch roof above me and the mountain beyond the highway and the vehicles moving and the faces in front of me.  I talked about chasing girls.  The translator said it and the audience laughed.  That was the crack.  That was the moment the assembly stopped being an assembly and became a conversation.  Laughter does that.  It did it in the chief's conference room when he said he would take whatever we had.  It did it on the stage at Bucal High School when a foreigner admitted through a translator that he used to chase girls and a few hundred students recognized something human in the man at the microphone.

After the laughter I told them the rest.

I told them I was an addict.  Not was in the way people say was when they mean it is behind them and neatly folded.  Was in the way that means I am one bad day from being one again.  I told them I was a dealer.  That I sold the thing that destroys families and I did it because it made me powerful and the power was more addictive than the product.  I told them I was an abuser.  That my drinking became blacking out and my blacking out became violence and I called my wife a liar when she showed me what I had done because admitting it would mean I was the monster and monsters do not get to keep the life they built.  I told them I chose a bottle over my wife and she left and the separation lasted years.  I told them I was homeless.  That the distance between the man everyone feared and the man everyone stepped over was shorter than they could imagine and I had walked it in both directions.

I told them I carried methamphetamine.  I told them I was arrested.  I told them about the places a person ends up when every choice they make is the wrong one and they make it anyway because the wrong choice is the only one that feels like a choice at all.

Then I told them Jesus found me at the bottom of all of it and did not ask what I had done.  He asked what I wanted to become.

That is who was standing on a stage in the Philippines telling teenagers not to follow his path.  Not a counselor.  Not a teacher.  Not a man with a degree and a program.  An addict.  A dealer.  An abuser.  A man who had done every single thing these students were being recruited toward and had survived it only because somebody did not quit on him.

I did not frame it as a warning.  Warnings are enforcement.  I framed it as a desperate plea.  Do not follow along our paths.  The consequences are dire.  I was not a teacher telling them what not to do.  I was a man who had done all of it telling them what it cost.

The translator carried every word and I watched the faces change.

Here is what I know about shame that I did not learn from a book.  If you live in shame of the pain you have caused others, that shame will make you accept the pain others put onto you.  You tell yourself you deserve it.  You hurt people, so you should be hurt.  You caused damage, so damage is what you get.  The shame becomes a door that opens inward and lets everything in.  The hard way walks right through that door.  It says you are guilty and the punishment is whatever we decide it is and you will accept it because you already believe you deserve it.

We told those students that we accept we should be virtuous.  But when it comes time to be virtuous we are not perfect at it.  Nobody is.  Recovery includes relapse.  A lot of it.  The distance between the bottom and the top is not a straight line, it is a mess of setbacks and failures and mornings where you wake up and you are back at the start.  That is the truth.  And a system that gives you one chance — one surrender, one shot at compliance, one strike before the farm or the cell or the bullet — that system is missing grace.  That system has no room for the actual shape of a human life, which is not a line from bad to good but a scrawl that doubles back on itself a hundred times before it finds a direction.

The hard way gives you one chance.  The most excellent way gives you grace.  That is the difference.  That is the whole difference.

There is a difference between a student hearing don't do drugs from a teacher in a uniform and hearing I did drugs and I relapsed and I relapsed again and I am standing here from a man who is visibly on the other side of it.  One is enforcement.  The other is honesty.  The students can tell the difference in about three seconds.

But we were not there for the students.  Not primarily.  We were there for the teachers standing along the walls and sitting in the back rows.  The ones watching us do something they had never been trained to do.  We were showing them that vulnerability in front of a classroom is not weakness.  That a student who hears the truth from an honest adult will give you more than a student who hears a rule from an enforcer.  That the pattern can change.  Enforcement builds walls.  Honesty opens doors.  We were not there to give one assembly.  We were there to change how the adults in that building talked to the young people in it for every day after we left.

The program was called Morals but the real curriculum was the method.  Stand up.  Tell the truth.  Do not be ashamed of the terrible things because Jesus renewed you and forgave you and sent you here to show that nobody is broken beyond fix.  If the teachers could learn that — if they could stand in front of their own students and be honest about their own lives rather than hiding behind authority — then the pattern of enforcement that ran through every institution in this country, from the presidential palace to the police station to the classroom, could be interrupted.

One school at a time.  The same way the hard way spread, one institution at a time.

Matt and Josh and I stood on a stage with a thatch roof and a view of six-wheeled vehicles on the highway and we knocked and we pleaded.  But not in the way they were used to.  When Duterte's men knocked, they kicked in doors at two in the morning and left bodies on the pavement.  When we knocked, we opened our own door first and let them see what was inside.  We pleaded — not for surrender, but for a chance.  Give these kids a chance.  Give these teachers a chance to try something other than enforcement.  Give us a chance to prove that nobody is broken beyond fix.  Tokhang took the most human words in the language — knock, plead — and made them into instruments of death.  We were taking them back.

That is the most excellent way.  Not a program.  A pattern.  Honesty rather than enforcement.  It works on a police chief in a conference room.  It works on sixty men behind bars.  It works on teenagers in an assembly.  It works on teachers watching from the back of the room.  It works because it is the opposite of everything the hard way does.

The hard way says you are broken and we will break you further until you comply.

The most excellent way says I was broken and I am not anymore and neither are you.

I remember walking off that stage and seeing the teachers talking to each other.  Not to us.  To each other.  That was the moment I knew it had worked.  We were not the point.  We were the demonstration.  The teachers were already figuring out how to do it themselves.

The six-wheeled vehicles were still moving on the highway when we left.  The mountain had not moved.  Nothing outside the school had changed.  But inside it, a group of adults had just watched three Americans stand in front of their students and be more honest than any authority figure those kids had ever encountered.  And instead of the students losing respect, they leaned in.

Enforcement would not have gotten that.  Honesty did.

One day we went to the mall.  It was already Christmas there, the tree up in early November, and we ate lunch.  My translator's fiance met us.  He worked in real estate.  Over the meal he started showing me what my money could buy in the Philippines.  Not Hope Dealer money.  Not ministry money.  My regular American salary.  The homes that cost a million pesos looked like something out of a magazine and a million pesos was nothing on a programmer's income.  I kept telling him I did not want to see the nice ones.  Show me the slums.  I wanted to plant a ministry, not live behind a gate.

That conversation was when privilege clicked for me.  I had been in Southblock 7 where dinner is one package of ramen split five ways.  I had been in the warehouse store buying cart loads because we could.  I knew the math of poverty.  But sitting in that mall with a real estate agent showing me listings, I saw the other side of the math.  My normal was their extraordinary.  The same paycheck that makes me middle class in the States could fund a church for a year, feed a neighborhood, keep a recovery program running.  I did not earn that math.  I was born into it.  And once you see it you cannot unsee it.

I seriously considered relocating.  I did the numbers.  I looked at what it would take to move to the Philippines and plant something permanent in the places the hard way had gutted.  I wanted to be the one who did not fly home.  I wanted to be the one who stayed, like Leonardo on the hill, like Pastor Anthony and RCGGM in Calauan.

I did not move.  I came home.  But the math stayed with me.  It is still there for anyone who wants to do it.

The stage at Bucal was behind us.  The doors in Southblock 7 were behind us.  But the doors at city hall were next, and behind those doors sat a man whose wife had been saying everything we were saying long before we got on a plane.


# CHAPTER.005 — Whatever You Have

Outside city hall there was a beautiful Mercedes SUV parked by the entrance and a woman selling food on the sidewalk next to it.  Three feet between the two Philippines.  The air-conditioned office and the street vendor.  The mayor's car and the woman's cart.  James warned us not to eat what she was selling because most street food was unsafe.  I thought about that later.  The system will walk you into a basement where sixty men are being beaten in a side room but it will also warn you not to eat a woman's cooking on the sidewalk.  The priorities tell you everything about who the system was built to protect.

City hall was one of the few air-conditioned spaces on the entire trip.  I remember that before I remember anything about the mayor.  After days of sweating through Hope Dealer shirts in classrooms and churches and holding cells, the cold air hit like walking into a different country.  The Philippines disappeared for a second and you were standing in an office that could have been in any midsize American city.  Nice furniture.  Flags.  The trappings of a man who held office and wanted you to know it.

There was a line to see him.  Mayor Justin Marc Chipeco — Timmy, they called him — was a busy man in a tight spot.  Duterte's drug war was pressing down from Manila.  The surrenderees were pressing up from the streets.  The chief was pressing in from the station.  The mayor sat in the middle of all of it, handling the pressure the way a Catholic handles pressure, which in my experience means enduring it and probably drinking.  He had the bearing of a politician who was used to people wanting things from him and had developed the facial expression to match.  He was polite.  He was measured.  He listened with the patience of a man who had listened to a hundred pitches and expected ours to sound like the rest.

His wife had already done the work.

We met with the mayor privately, separate from the council, which was good because he would not have said what he said next in front of his officials.  He told me about his wife.  Her name was Laverne.  Laverne Chipeco.  She was born again.  He was Catholic.  In a Catholic country with a machismo culture, a wife telling her husband to try a different way is a woman talking into a wall.  She had been telling him about faith that reaches people, about recovery, about something other than enforcement.  She had been saying it at the dinner table, in the kitchen, wherever wives say the things husbands do not want to hear.  For months.  Probably years.  And the wall did what walls do.  It stood there.

Then five knuckleheads who cannot step off a dock without gashing a shin flew across the Pacific and told a machismo politician the same things his wife had been saying.  And he listened.

A woman tells her husband there is another way and he does not hear it.  Five Americans with tattoos and Hope Dealer shirts walk into his air-conditioned office and say the same words and suddenly it carries weight.  That is both funny and infuriating and it is the truth about how power works.  The institution gives weight to certain voices and not others.  The mayor's wife had the message.  She did not have the microphone.  We walked through a door she built and acted like we built it.

The anonymous hero of this book is Laverne Chipeco, a born-again woman married to a Catholic mayor who never stopped telling him there was another way.  We did not bring the most excellent way to Calamba.  She did.  We just showed up loud enough that he finally heard her.

The city council met in a chamber with the Philippine flag behind them and officials in white barongs filling the seats.  We were a handful of guys in black polos in the back of the room.  The image tells you everything about the arrangement.  An entire municipal government listening to five Americans who came with nothing but themselves.

I expected resistance.  I expected the practiced disinterest of politicians who had already decided what they thought before we opened our mouths.  What I got was engagement.  Real engagement.  The council members leaned forward.  They asked questions.  They were astonished.

The astonishment, I think, was about the transparency.  They had seen presentations before.  NGOs came through Calamba with binders and budgets and exit strategies.  Aid workers showed up with logos on their shirts and metrics on their slides.  Every one of them had a pitch that was polished enough to hide whatever they were actually after.  We had no binder.  We had no budget to speak of.  We had three former addicts and two accountants and a message that had not changed since we sat down across from the chief of police.

We said the same thing in the council chamber that we said in the conference room and at Bucal High School and in the holding cells and door to door in Southblock 7.  The message did not change because it did not need to change.  The chief needed to hear it.  The students needed to hear it.  The men behind the bars needed to hear it.  The council needed to hear it.  The room got bigger and the words stayed the same.  That was not a strategy.  That was all we had.

The council signed on.  Councilor Leeanne Cortez, who chaired the Committee on Health and Social Services, became the legislative engine.  She was the one who would turn whatever we started into ordinances and funding and city policy.  But I think what moved them was the same thing that moved the chief.  Not the pitch.  The people making it.  Three men who had been exactly where the surrenderees were, standing in front of a government body, upright and breathing and making eye contact.  The evidence was not in a binder.  The evidence was at the table.

Kirby John Kraft is a hero of this story.

I know what it looked like in the earlier chapters.  A man who ran holding cells where sixty bodies crammed into six bunkbeds.  A man whose station had a room where the crying does not stop unless visitors come downstairs.  A man who answered to a system that measured success in bodies on the pavement.  I need you to understand something about that man that I did not understand when I first walked into his conference room.

He was looking for a way out.  Not for himself.  For everyone.

Kraft was the Acting Provincial Director of the Laguna Provincial Police Office.  He did not run one station.  He ran every station in the province.  Every six-wheeled vehicle in Laguna answered to him.  Every barangay operation, every watch list, every holding cell from Calamba to Calauan to Santa Cruz — his jurisdiction.  Manila told him to be strong and strong meant bodies.  Duterte had built a system where a police chief's value was measured by how many problems he eliminated, and eliminated did not mean rehabilitated.  Kraft had quotas to meet and a president who did not care how he met them.  His officers were carrying out operations that broke them in ways nobody would talk about for years.  The killing was destroying his force from the inside.  Every masked officer on a six-wheeled vehicle was a man who went home at night and lived with what he had done.  Kraft saw this happening and he could not stop it because stopping it meant defying Manila and defying Manila meant losing his command and losing his command meant someone worse taking his place.

He was not looking for a photo opportunity.  He was looking for an exit.  Not an escape from accountability.  An escape from the hard way itself.  He wanted a different way to be strong and nobody in Manila was offering one.

When he sat across from us in that conference room, he was not evaluating a proposal.  He was looking at five men and trying to determine if we were real.  Real in the specific way that mattered to a man in his position.  Not whether we believed what we said.  Whether we would still be there in six months.

He was tired of people showing up, making a scene, and leaving.  NGOs came.  Mission trippers came.  Aid workers with cameras and good intentions came.  They shook hands and took photos and the airplane took them home and the chief was still sitting in the same station with the same cells and the same mandate from Manila.  Every person who showed up and left made his situation worse because each departure proved what Manila already believed.  Addicts cannot be fixed.  Nothing works.  Go back to the hard way.

So when we told him we are not leaving you, that was the sentence that mattered.  Not the presentation.  Not the stories about our own recovery.  The commitment.  We may go home but we stay here.  The network stays.  RCGGM stays.  The pastoral coalition stays.  The funded church stays.  When our airplane leaves, the people on the ground remain.  When the Hope Dealer shirts fade, the relationships do not.  We are not leaving you.

He said he would take whatever we had.  The room laughed.  But I think now that the laugh was relief more than humor.  The photo we took of him shows it.  He is holding a Hope Dealer shirt with a slight smile on his face and the smile is not pride.  It is the expression of a man who has been holding his breath for a very long time and someone just told him he could exhale.  He was tall for a Filipino.  Clean-shaven.  Full police uniform.  He commanded the room.  But in that photo he is holding a t-shirt from five strangers like it is a life preserver.

The staircase he took us down was not a display of power.  It was a confession.  He showed us the worst thing happening under his authority because nobody else who had walked into his station had talked about addicts like they were people.  When he ducked his head into that side room and the crying stopped, that was not performance.  That was a man who wanted it to stop.  My prayer was not fighting him.  It was with him.  He needed our presence in that room as much as the men in the cells did.

The pastoral coalition gathered at one of the local churches.  A Philippine Christian ministers fellowship.  Inter-denominational.  The invitation went to every pastor in the area and the responses sorted themselves along a line that surprised me and did not surprise me at all.

The Catholics said no.

The institutional church, the one with the most buildings and the most members and the most history on the island, looked at us with disdain. # CHAPTER.006 — Contagion

The barangay hall in Pansol had plastic chairs and a microphone that squealed when you stood too close to the speaker.  The room was government-issued in every detail.  Fluorescent lights, a Philippine flag on a stand, a folding table at the front for the officials.  The men in the chairs wore blue shirts.  Surrenderees.  The blue identified them the way the masks identified the officers on the six-wheeled vehicles, except the blue was meant to mark compliance and the masks were meant to erase identity.  The room was packed.  Men sat shoulder to shoulder in rows that went back farther than I expected.  Some stood along the walls.  The air had the weight of too many bodies in a space designed for municipal meetings about drainage and zoning.

Something felt wrong about the room and I could not place my finger on it.  I was at the front.  Everyone faced me.  The officers in the back were reaching toward me.  The surrenderees in the rows were looking up at me.  I had been in their chairs.  I had been in their cells.  I had been the man in the row looking up at someone who did not know what it felt like.  And now I was standing where that person stood and something about the arrangement was telling a lie I could not name yet.

The government organized these meetings.  The same apparatus that ran Tokhang, that compiled the watch lists, that sent the six-wheeled vehicles into neighborhoods between ten at night and four in the morning, was now convening recovery sessions in barangay halls and seating the survivors in plastic chairs.  The state that created the problem was hosting the solution.  The officers who drove the vehicles stood along the walls of the same room where the men they had processed sat in rows.  There was no separation between the machinery of the hard way and the practice of the most excellent way.  They shared a building and a budget and a government seal on the wall.  I remember looking at the officers along the walls and the surrenderees in the chairs and thinking: six months ago one of these groups was dragging the other out of their houses at two in the morning.  Now they shared a room and a microphone and a government seal.

The format was The Most Excellent Way.  We start every session the same way: scripture is the answer.  If you come here and we give you opinion, don't come back.  As long as we keep going back to what God says about us and not what man's opinion is, we are not wasting your time.  That is the rule.  The only authority in the circle is the word.  Not the facilitator.  Not the man who has been clean the longest.  Not the officer along the wall.  Scripture.  Scripture as the backbone.  The beatitudes as the framework.  Blessed are the poor in spirit.  Blessed are those who mourn.  Blessed are the meek.  I had read those words in churches in Salem Heights where they sounded like aspirations, virtues you were supposed to cultivate, spiritual goals on a checklist.  In a barangay hall full of surrenderees they were not aspirations.  They were descriptions.  The men in the blue shirts were the poor in spirit.  They were the ones who mourned.  They were the meek.  The beatitudes were not telling them what to become.  The beatitudes were telling them what they already were and that what they already were was blessed.  That word landed in that room like a detonation.  Blessed.  Men who had been told by their own government that they were unfixable, that they were a logistics problem, that the best solution for them was the farm or the bullet or the cell — those men were hearing that their poverty of spirit was not a deficiency.  It was the qualification.

We had Tagalog materials.  Salem Heights Church made them and donated them to the ministry.  James translated them.  Scripture-based sessions, thirty to forty-five minutes each, built around themes: identity, victory, defeat, death, addiction, transformation.  Grounded in the premise that identity in Christ defeats death and addiction and the hard way and transforms lives.  We brought them on a USB stick that I uploaded to SharePoint, but they were downloaded and printed before we left the States.  Physical pages.  Tagalog.  Something any peer leader could pick up and run a session with after we flew home.  Each card paired a scripture story with one of the ten attitudes of victorious living drawn from the beatitudes.  Stories like the demoniac at the pool of Bethesda — the man waiting to be put in the water because nobody would carry him, and Jesus asking the only question that matters: do you want to be made well.  One story for each attitude.  Everything you need to run a circle fits on a trifold handout — the beatitudes, Jude 3:14, the promises of God.  One folded piece of paper.  No binder.  The full curriculum is available at tmewcf.org for anyone who wants to build a circle of their own.  The session is simple.  Scripture is read.  Leaders break the circle into small groups.  You talk about how it hit you.  Keep it about the scripture, not about opinion.  Pray for the group.  Done.  That is the entire program.  No certification required.  No degree.  A trifold, a circle, and the willingness to let the word do the work instead of the man holding it.  The honesty was ours.  The materials were transferable.  That combination is what made it contagion.

The program was called the Moral Recovery Program.  The city would build it into something larger — Sipag at Tiyaga, Diligence and Perseverance, the signature community-based drug rehabilitation program that became Calamba's answer to the hard way.  It had a name and a document and an MOA signed on November 7, 2018, at the Eduardo Barretto Sr. Elementary School Quadrangle in Barangay Pansol.  RCGGM adopted the school.  That gave it a permanent home.  But the program was not the document.  The program was what happened in the room when someone stood at the front and told the truth.

We went to at least four barangay meetings.  Pansol, Mayapa, others I cannot name with certainty because the rooms started to blend.  The same plastic chairs.  The same fluorescent lights.  The same blue shirts.  The format did not change because the message did not change.  Jesus is the solution.  Hope endures.  Nobody is too lost.  We said it in Pansol and said it in Mayapa and said it in rooms I remember by their walls and their heat and the faces in them but not by their official names.  The room got bigger but the words did not change.

The call went out in every room.  The same call we had made in the holding cells.  The same invitation.  And in every room, the hands went up.

Then the officers raised theirs.

I watched it happen and I did not expect it and I should have.  The chief had told us the killing was destroying his men.  He said it in the conference room like a man describing structural damage.  Not emotional damage, not moral damage — structural.  The machine was breaking the people who operated it.  His officers were carrying out Tokhang and the weight of what they were doing was cracking them in places they did not have language for.  And when the call went out in the barangay hall, the officers standing along the walls raised their hands the same way the surrenderees did.  The same way the men behind the bars did.  The same way the students at Bucal did.

Police officers.  The ones carrying out the hard way.  Reaching.

Nobody is broken beyond fix includes the ones holding the gun.  That is not a metaphor.  That is not a theological abstraction.  That is a man in uniform standing in the back of a room full of men he has processed through a system designed to destroy them, raising his hand because the system is destroying him too and someone just offered him something other than the hard way.

I led a call once.  One time.  They handed me the microphone and I stood at the front of a room full of blue shirts and I made the call and the room responded.

The response was powerful.  I felt it immediately.  The room moved toward me the way a room moves toward the person holding the mic when the words coming through it are true.  I felt every face in that room turn and I felt the energy of their attention gather into a single point and that point was me.  The hands went up and the sound in the room changed and for a moment I was the center of something larger than myself.

I recognized it.

I recognized it because I had felt it before.  Not in a church.  Not in a ministry.  I had felt it dealing.  The same electricity.  The same rush of having a room respond to you, of being the person everyone looks at, of controlling the energy of a space full of people.  When you deal, a neighborhood depends on you.  When you hold the mic and make the call and the hands go up, a room depends on you.  The power is the same power.  The substance is different but the high is identical.

I was afraid of it.  I say that plainly because a former addict who does not recognize a new addiction is a former addict who is about to relapse.  The most excellent way has its own trap door and I was standing on it.  The mic is the trap.  The response of the room is the trap.  The feeling that you are the one doing the healing, that you are the instrument, that without you the room would not have moved — that feeling is the trap.  It is the same feeling that kept me dealing long after the money stopped mattering.  The money was the excuse.  The power was the drug.  And the mic offered the same drug in a package that looked like ministry.

The most excellent way is not immune to corruption.  Ministry can become its own addiction.  The person at the front of the room can start needing the room the way the room needs them, and that need will bend everything around it.  It will make you hold the mic longer.  It will make you take the stage more often.  It will make you believe you are essential, and the moment you believe you are essential you have stopped practicing the most excellent way and started practicing the hard way with better language.  Because the hard way is about power.  It has always been about power.  Duterte at the podium.  The chief in his conference room.  The officer behind the mask.  The dealer on the corner.  The preacher at the mic.  Power is power regardless of what you are selling and power is the thing that an addict must handle the way a burn victim handles fire.

I gave the mic back.  That was the only time.

Blessed are the meek.  Not blessed are the ones with the mic.  The beatitudes are not just the framework for the surrenderees.  They are the guardrails for the person at the front of the room.  If you are not poor in spirit when you stand up there, you should not be standing up there.  If the mic makes you feel powerful instead of terrified, something has gone wrong.

They thought they lacked a system.  The chief sat at that table and said nobody could fix addicts.  He thought he needed a program, a manual, a set of instructions from Manila.  He thought the answer was structural.  A system.  A framework.  Something you could print and distribute and implement across jurisdictions.

They forgot that they themselves were the solution.

The surrenderees helping the surrenderees.  That was the answer.  Not a program imported from the States or a manual written by people who had never been inside a holding cell.  The people closest to the problem were the ones most capable of addressing it.  The men in the blue shirts knew things about addiction and recovery and relapse and the streets and the fear and the shame that no program manual could contain.  They knew it because they had lived it.  The system Calamba needed was already sitting in the plastic chairs.

Peer-led.  That is the clinical word for it.  I prefer the honest one.  Broken people helping broken people.  The man who surrendered last month sitting next to the man who surrendered yesterday and telling him what the first week looks like.  The man who relapsed sitting next to the man who has been clean for sixty days and neither one of them pretending the distance between them is permanent.  Recovery includes relapse.  A lot of it.  The distance between the bottom and the top is not a straight line and a system that gives you one chance — one surrender, one shot at compliance, one strike before the farm — that system is missing grace.  The hard way gives you one chance.  The most excellent way gives you the next morning.

That is the difference between the hard way and the most excellent way.  The hard way is a system that discards people who fail within it.  The most excellent way is a community that expects failure and meets it with the same words it met the first day.  Nobody is broken beyond fix.  Not the first time they hear it and not the fifth time and not the twentieth.  The message does not change because the truth does not change.

The contagion was the pattern, not the program.  It spread from Pansol to Mayapa to barangays whose names I did not write down because I was not thinking about a book, I was thinking about the faces.  The same plastic chairs.  The same fluorescent lights.  The same message.  The same call.  The rooms got bigger.  The hands kept going up.  Officers along the walls.  Surrenderees in the chairs.  The government organizing the meetings and the most excellent way filling them with something the government did not design and could not have designed because the government builds systems and the most excellent way is not a system.  It is people.

One room at a time.  The same way the hard way spread, operation by operation, neighborhood by neighborhood.  The most excellent way spread the same way.  Barangay to barangay.  Not because someone in Manila ordered it.  Because someone in a blue shirt told someone else in a blue shirt that there was a meeting on Tuesday and the words in it were true.  The contagion was not the program.  The contagion was the honesty.  One surrenderee telling another that he was not too far gone, and meaning it, because last month he had been exactly where the other man was standing.

They thought they lacked a system.  They forgot that they themselves were the solution.  The answer to the hard way was never going to come from a binder or a government program or five Americans on a ten-day trip.  The answer was in the holding cells.  It was in the blue shirts.  It was in the men who had been told they were unfixable sitting in plastic chairs in barangay halls and discovering that the person next to them had been told the same thing and was still breathing.  The infrastructure of recovery was not a document.  It was each other.

The rooms got bigger.  The message did not change.

On the last day we presented the Most Excellent Way to RCGGM as a model they could carry forward without us.  The meeting halls had been organized like classrooms — chairs in rows, the people with the solutions at the front, the surrenderees sitting and receiving.  The hard way arranged as furniture.  Rows say listen to us.  Rows say we have the answer and you do not.  Rows say the system is at the front and you are in it.

In the barangay meetings the surrenderees sat in rows facing the front.  The hard way's furniture.  The system put the authorities at the front because the system only knows hierarchy.  The surrenderees were not people yet.  They were an audience.  They were rows.

On the last day we showed RCGGM what a circle looked like.  In their own church we sat together — not us at the front and them receiving, but everyone facing each other.  Then we flew home.  And RCGGM took the circle to the barangays on their own.  We did not rearrange the furniture.  We showed them what the furniture could look like and they carried it into every room after we left.

That was the moment.  Not the MOA signing.  Not the beatitudes or the Tagalog materials or the mic.  The chairs.  When we stopped standing above them and sat down among them, the surrenderees turned to face each other instead of facing the front.  They stopped being an audience and became the program.  The circle says the answer is in this room.  The circle says look at the person next to you because he was where you are last month and he is still breathing.  The circle is peer-led drawn on a floor in plastic chairs.

Calamba already knew the circle.  The city had been practicing what some called the Circle of Good Governance — a leadership philosophy that moved away from hierarchical classroom-style meetings toward circular ones.  The city government already sat in circles.  What they had not done was fill that circle with surrenderees and tell them they were the solution.  We did not invent the furniture.  Calamba built the floor.  We brought the people who needed to sit in it.

That is when the model clicked for RCGGM.  That is when they saw it.  Not a curriculum to deliver from the front of a room but a circle to hold.  The furniture was the thesis.  They thought they lacked a system.  The system was a circle of chairs and the willingness to sit in them honestly.

The rooms got bigger.  The message did not change.  And the chairs faced each other.

And in every room, I noticed, people were reaching.  The same gesture I had seen in the basement.  Hands through bars, hands in the air, hands raised during the call.  It kept happening.  I did not yet understand what I was seeing.


# CHAPTER.007 — Hands

The kid was maybe thirteen.  It was night.  We were walking the streets near RCGGM in Calamba, doing what we did — talking to anyone who would stop, telling them about Jesus.  The kid stopped.  He listened.  He had that look kids get when they are hearing something for the first time that matches something they already suspected.  He was small and serious and he accepted Christ right there on the sidewalk.

Two teenagers sat outside a building maybe thirty feet away.  They started shouting at him.  Come back.  Get over here.  They were not his friends in the way that matters.  They were gangsters, or close enough.  The kind of older boys who own a younger boy's schedule and call it loyalty.  They were not asking.

The kid stood between us.  The entire war for a human soul was happening on a residential sidewalk in the Philippines at nine o'clock at night.  On one side, two men in Hope Dealer shirts saying nobody is broken beyond fix.  On the other, two teenagers who had already decided what this kid was going to become.  The most excellent way pulling one direction.  The hard way pulling the other.  A child in the middle who had just said yes to Jesus and now had to walk home past the boys who heard him say it.

I do not know what happened to that kid.  I have thought about him more than almost anyone else in this book.  I do not know if he went back to the teenagers.  I do not know if the prayer held or if the sidewalk swallowed it.  I do not know his name.  I know his face and I know he was reaching for something and I know two other sets of hands were reaching for him at the same time.

That is the image I cannot put down.  Hands.  Everyone in this story is reaching.

The women's prison was not like the men's holding cells.  It was smaller.  Ten to twenty women, roughly as many beds.  Still a cage, still bars, still the thick stale heat of a Philippine detention facility.  But the bodies were not stacked three deep.  There was room to breathe in there.  Whether that made it better or worse is a question I am not qualified to answer.

We stood outside the cell with a loudspeaker.  The bars between us and them.  We shared testimonies from the other side, the same brutal honesty we brought everywhere — addiction, violence, what Jesus did with the wreckage.  But here I had to go further than I had gone anywhere else.

I told them about my wife.

My drinking became blacking out and my blacking out became abusive.  At first I tried to deny it.  Then I fought it, calling my wife a liar.  But bruises and scars do not lie.  When she offered me a choice between her and drinking, it was too easy to drink.  That is what addiction does.  It takes the person you are supposed to love more than anything and makes them lighter than a bottle.  I chose the bottle.  She left.  The separation lasted years.

I stood on the wrong side of the bars and told them that through a loudspeaker.  Every word of it.  The drinking, the blackouts, the abuse I denied, the wife I called a liar, the bruises I pretended were not there, the choice I made wrong.  I told women in a cage the kind of man I had been and some of those women were likely in that cage because of men exactly like the one I was describing.  I told them Jesus washed it clean — not erased it, not pretended it did not happen, but took the filth of what I had done and made something new out of what was left.  I told them my wife came back.

Telling it to the students at the recovery programs cost me something.  But telling it to women in a prison cell — women who had been on the receiving end of men like the one I used to be — through a loudspeaker, through a translator, through bars — cost me everything I had.  The translator was a woman.  She carried my confession from English to Tagalog without flinching.  I do not know what that cost her either.

Something shifted.  Not in a dramatic way.  Not a collapse or a shout.  Something in the air between the bars changed.  The women listened and the distance closed.  I was not a visitor anymore.  I was a man who had done what men do to women, standing outside their cage admitting it, saying there is a way out of being that person.  The most excellent way is not a program for good people.  It is a program for people like me.  I was the evidence.

They let us in.

That is not something you earn quickly.  You do not walk up to a cage full of women and get invited inside.  You earn it by breaking your own heart in front of them first.  We broke our hearts through a loudspeaker and through bars and they watched us do it and then whoever made the decision — the guards, the women themselves, I do not know — decided we could come inside.

One of the women grabbed Matt's butt.

I am putting that in this book because it happened and because it matters.  We had just been let inside after pouring out the worst things about ourselves and then immediately the room was light with laughter because a woman in a Philippine prison cell decided that Matt looked grabbable and she went for it.  Matt turned red.  The women laughed.  We laughed.  The guards probably laughed.  That moment did more for the humanity in that cell than anything I said into the loudspeaker.  These women were not tragic.  They were not broken creatures waiting to be saved by Americans with microphones.  They were people — people who flirted, people who laughed, people who had been locked up long enough to know that if you cannot find something funny inside a cage you will not survive it.  The grab said: we are still here.  We are still us.  Do not mistake this cell for the end of us.

This was the moment — the one we had earned through the loudspeaker and the confession and the breaking of hearts through bars.  In that photo the bars disappear.  You cannot tell who is the prisoner and who is the visitor.  Four men who had been on the wrong side of every law they encountered, arms linked with women who were currently on the wrong side.  The distance between us was nothing.  The distance had always been nothing.  The bars were the lie.

You will have to imagine it.  Arms linked.  Smiling.  The cage around all of us and none of us belonging in it.

I remember Matt's face.  I remember the weight of my own words coming back at me in Tagalog from a woman who had to hold them in her mouth long enough to translate them.  The rest I carry as a feeling, not a film.

Carla was back home and he needed to check in, to tell her we were OK, to relive some of what we had just experienced with the person who knew him best. He did not need to. The athletes were the lightest scene in the trip and maybe the most important.

Kids playing ball on a concrete basketball court under a covered pavilion with low walls open to the air. It does not recruit.  It shows up as something you need and by the time you realize what it actually is, you have already let it in.

I watched those kids eat Jollibee hamburgers and I thought about the thirteen-year-old on the sidewalk.  This field was the alternative.  This was the place that kid should have been instead of on a dark street being shouted at by gangsters.  Every hamburger on that field was a hand reaching for a kid before the street could reach him first.

A way out.

An escape from the hard way.

The hard way is a fist.  It closes around people and it does not let go.  Tokhang was a closed fist.  The farm was a closed fist.  The six-wheeled vehicles driving the streets at night were closed fists.  The system that says addicts cannot be fixed, that the poor are disposable, that strength means bodies — that is a fist, and it has been closed around communities like Southblock 7 and holding cells like the one beneath the Calamba police station for as long as men with power have decided that other men are not worth saving.

The most excellent way is an open hand.

That is all it is.  An open hand reaching toward a closed fist and saying: you can let go.  Nobody is broken beyond fix.  Not the men behind the bars.  Not the women in the cell.  Not the kid on the sidewalk.  Not the chief who wanted to stop the crying.  Not the officers who raised their hands in the barangay meetings because the killing was destroying them too.  Not the gangsters shouting at a thirteen-year-old to come back.  Not even them.

The whole book is hands.  I did not plan it that way.  I just kept writing about what I saw, and what I saw, every time, in every room and on every street and in every cell, was people reaching.  Reaching for hope.  Reaching for someone who would not quit on them.  Reaching for proof that the hard way was not the only way.

Some of those hands I held.  Some I could not reach.  Some I held for a moment and then the airplane brought me home and I do not know if they are still reaching or if someone else took hold.  The kid on the sidewalk.  The women behind the bars.  The men three rows deep.

I think about their hands.  I think about them all the time.

Those hands were reaching for something beyond us.  They were reaching, though they did not know it yet, for a reckoning.


# CHAPTER.008 — The Dock

The International Criminal Court sits in The Hague, in the Netherlands, in a building designed to look like justice.  Glass and steel and clean lines.  It is the kind of building where the temperature is always correct.  The air does not smell like sixty men.  There are no fans bolted to the walls.  There is no crying in the side room.  It is as far from the basement of a police station in Calamba as architecture can get.

Rodrigo Duterte called it bullshit.

He said he would rot in jail before he answered to a foreign court.  He said the ICC was useless.  He said what every strongman says when the machinery of accountability starts moving in his direction: that it is imperialism, that it is politics, that the people who are coming for him do not understand what he was dealing with.  The drug problem was real.  The threat was real.  He saved the country.  That is the script.  It has not changed since Nuremberg.  The details shift but the defense is always the same.  I had no choice.  They made me do it.  The ones I killed deserved it.

The ones he killed were the men behind the bars I prayed over.  The ones in the side room where the crying stopped.  The surrenderees in the holding cells and the bodies on the pavement with cardboard signs and the seventeen-year-old named Kian delos Santos who was dragged into an alley by three police officers and shot.  Kian was seventeen.  The officers said he fought back.  The security camera said he did not.  In November 2022, while I was processing what I had seen in Calamba exactly four years earlier, three officers were convicted of killing him.  It was the only domestic conviction to come out of the entire drug war.  One boy.  Three officers.  Thousands of dead.  One conviction.

That is what domestic accountability looked like in the Philippines.  One case, out of what human rights organizations estimate at twelve thousand to thirty thousand killings.  One.

The ICC exists for the gap between one and thirty thousand.

---

The timeline moves the way justice moves.  Slowly.  In February 2018, the ICC prosecutor opened a preliminary examination into the killings.  The following month, Duterte pulled the Philippines out of the Rome Statute.  The withdrawal was theater.  It took a year to take effect and the court retained jurisdiction over everything that happened between November 2011 and March 2019.  The Davao Death Squad.  Tokhang.  The holding cells.  The farm.  All of it still within the court's reach because you cannot withdraw from a treaty and take your crimes with you.

In September 2021, the Pre-Trial Chamber authorized a formal investigation.  The charge was crimes against humanity.  Article 7.  Murder.  Not isolated incidents.  Not rogue officers.  A widespread and systematic attack on a civilian population pursuant to state policy.  The language is precise because international law has to be precise when it is accusing a president of orchestrating mass killing.  Widespread means it was not an accident.  Systematic means it was not a mistake.  State policy means it came from the top.

Two months later the investigation was suspended.  The Philippines claimed it was handling things domestically.  The court waited.  In January 2023 the ICC rejected the deferral.  The domestic proceedings were insufficient.  Of course they were.  You do not ask the machine to investigate itself and expect it to find fault in its own engineering.  The investigation resumed.

By July 2023, Prosecutor Karim Khan confirmed active evidence-gathering.  Witnesses testified from outside the Philippines because testifying from inside meant dying.  The RISE UP coalition — mothers and widows of the dead — cooperated with the investigation at a cost I cannot calculate from the safety of my desk.  These women buried their sons and husbands and then walked into rooms and said his name and what was done to him and who did it.  They knew their name was on a list now.  And they knew how their government managed lists of names.  The surrenderees had put their names on a list and it made them more vulnerable, not less.  These mothers did the same thing — surrendered their anonymity to the ICC — knowing exactly what surrender had cost the last million people who tried it.  I remember the hands in the basement reaching through the bars.  These mothers reached too, through a different kind of bar — the bar of witness testimony, of legal proceedings, of saying the truth to a foreign court while their own country tried to silence them.

On March 11, 2025, Rodrigo Duterte landed at Ninoy Aquino International Airport after attending a political rally in Hong Kong.  He was still campaigning.  Still active.  Still the man the people allegedly wanted.  The Philippine National Police and Interpol arrested him at the airport in an operation called Pursuit.  He was held at Villamor Air Base and transferred to The Hague within twenty-four hours.  His daughter Sara said he was forced onto the plane.  The man who told his police to kill a thousand people and promised to protect them was the first leader from Asia to face trial before the International Criminal Court.  The dock is not empty anymore.

---

But the dock is not the point.  The dock is where the story ends.  The point is that the story does not end.

Argentina's Dirty War killed thirty thousand people.  It took more than thirty years for the convictions to come.  The Khmer Rouge emptied Cambodia's cities and filled its mass graves and the tribunal did not deliver its first conviction until 2010, more than three decades after the killing fields.  In Rwanda, Felicien Kabuga financed a genocide in 1994 and was not arrested until 2020.  Twenty-six years.  Radovan Karadzic hid for thirteen years after the Bosnian War.  Ratko Mladic hid for sixteen.  Both were found.  Both were convicted.

The pattern is not speed.  The pattern is inevitability.  Once proceedings begin, almost no one permanently escapes.  The timeline stretches.  The fugitives age.  The governments change.  But the court does not forget and the witnesses do not stop testifying and the evidence does not expire.  Justice is patient in the way that only institutions without elections can be patient.  No one votes on whether to continue pursuing crimes against humanity.  The mandate outlasts the news cycle.

The pattern held.  Thirty years for Argentina.  Thirty for Cambodia.  Twenty-six for Rwanda.  Nine for Duterte.  The strongmen hide and the courts wait and the witnesses keep testifying and eventually the dock is not empty anymore.  March 11, 2025.  An airport.  Handcuffs.  The man who told his police to kill a thousand people and promised to protect them was arrested by police who were no longer protecting him.  He awaits his mayoral candidacy if he is ever released.  The people, allegedly, still want him.  The hard way does not end when the handcuffs close.  A democracy that learned to cheer for death does not unlearn it because a court in The Hague issues a warrant.  The dock can hold the man.  It cannot hold the appetite that put him in power.  That appetite is still there, in the Philippines and in America, waiting for the next person who promises to be strong.  His daughter Sara is the vice president.  His son is starting right where he left off, ensuring the power balance never changes.  The hard way is a family business.  You arrest the father and the son sits down in the same chair and the machine does not even pause.  The dock can hold Duterte.  It cannot hold the dynasty.  It cannot hold the idea that strength means bodies and the poor are disposable and the right last name is a license to continue.  That is not a problem a court can solve.  That is a problem only the most excellent way can solve, one barangay meeting at a time, one hand raised at a time, one officer at a time choosing a different way.  The court removes the man.  The people have to remove the appetite.

But here is something I did not know until after I started writing this book.  The man who held the Hope Dealer shirt in Calamba, the man who took us to the basement, the man who laughed and said he would take whatever we had — Kirby John Kraft was transferred to Davao.  He became the City Director of the Davao City Police Office.  The birthplace of the hard way.  The city where the death squads started.  The city Duterte plans to return to as mayor when he is released.  And the most excellent way got there first.  The man who watched sixty men reach through bars in his own holding cell was running the police in Duterte's hometown before the dynasty could reclaim it.  I did not plan that.  Kraft did not plan that.  But the God who sent five knuckleheads from Salem Heights to a conference room in Laguna is the same God who sent the man in that conference room to Davao.  The most excellent way does not stay where you plant it.  It follows the hard way to its source.

Kraft spent three years in Davao.  Three years is not a rotation.  It is a tenure.  Long enough to change the culture, train the officers, embed the approach.  Whatever the most excellent way looked like in Laguna, he had three years to plant it in the city where the hard way was born.  And then he was promoted.  Not fired.  Not forced out.  Promoted.  To Metro Manila.  Then Mindanao.  Then national headquarters at Camp Crame, where he now manages the personnel and records of every police officer in the Philippines.  A one-star general.  The man who said he would take whatever we had climbed from a provincial office to the desk that decides where every officer in the country goes and what they do when they get there.

The most excellent way did not stay in a conference room in Laguna.  It followed Kraft to Davao, where it had three years to work before the dynasty tries to return.  The officers he trained are still there.  The culture he built does not leave when he does.  The contagion climbed the ranks.  It is still climbing.

---

I was in Calamba in November 2018.  The same month the officers who killed Kian delos Santos went to trial.  I did not know it at the time.  I was in the basement and on the streets and in the school and at the doors in Southblock 7 and I did not know that somewhere in Manila a courtroom was doing the only thing a courtroom in the Philippines would do about thirty thousand dead.  One case.  One boy.  While I was watching sixty men reach through bars, a country was putting exactly three officers on trial and calling it accountability.

I think about the chief.  The man who sat at the head of the conference table and said he would take whatever we had.  The man who walked us into the basement and showed us the cells and told the crying to stop.  Where does he fall?  He is not Duterte.  He is not the architect.  He is not the man who went on television and told police officers to kill.  But he ran the station.  He managed the cells.  He sent his officers out in the six-wheeled vehicles with masks on their faces.  He carried the weight of what Manila demanded and he carried it all the way down to that basement.

I know what Kraft did with what he was given.  He showed us the cells when he did not have to.  He laughed when we offered ourselves.  He watched sixty men raise their hands to a call he could not make himself, and I saw in his eyes that he was watching something he had been praying for.  He was carrying the weight of a system he did not build, and when five strangers walked in and offered him a different way, he took it.  He took it and he carried it from Laguna to Davao to national headquarters.  That is not complicity.  That is a man who chose the most excellent way from inside the machine and spent the next seven years proving it worked at every level they sent him to.

But here is where I must be careful with my own heart.  The officers who raised their hands in the barangay meetings are part of the same system the ICC is investigating.  The men who drove the six-wheeled vehicles and wore the masks are the same men who stood along the walls and reached for Jesus when the call went out.  If I celebrate their hands raised in one chapter and call for their prosecution in another, I have abandoned the most excellent way.  Grace has to reach them too.  Justice and grace are not enemies.  The dock exists for the architects, for the ones who built the machine and ordered it to kill.  But the officers who operated it — the ones the chief said were being destroyed by what they were asked to do — they were reaching for a way out.  I saw their hands go up.  I will not pretend I did not see that.  The most excellent way does not pick who gets grace.  The men behind the bars got it.  The women in the prison got it.  The chief got it.  The officers get it too.  Justice for the system.  Grace for the people trapped inside it.  That is the paradox and I will not resolve it because it is not mine to resolve.  It is God's.

The ICC investigation covers the period from November 2011 to March 2019.  The chief was operating within that window.  Whether investigators have looked at Calamba, whether the chief's name appears in any file in The Hague, I do not know.  What I know is that the Calamba Moral Recovery Program — the program that grew from our visit, the program that sent surrenderees to help other surrenderees, the program that won awards — proves something the prosecution needs proved.  An alternative existed.  A humane alternative.  It was available.  It worked.  People recovered.  People who were supposed to be unfixable got fixed.  And the killing continued anyway.

That is the moral case.  Not just that people died, but that they did not have to.  The hard way was a choice.  Every body on the pavement with a cardboard sign was a choice made by a government that had another option and refused it.  The Calamba Model is evidence not in the legal sense but in the human sense.  It is the proof that the most excellent way was sitting right there at the table, offering itself, and the machine chose the bullet instead.

---

Senator Leila de Lima tried to stop it from the inside.  She led Senate hearings on the killings.  She brought witnesses.  She named names.  In February 2017, she was arrested on drug charges that anyone paying attention understood were political.  She spent nearly seven years in detention.  Seven years.  A senator.  Locked up for doing the thing senators are supposed to do, which is hold power accountable.  The message was not subtle.  Investigate the killings and you become one of the prisoners.  De Lima's detention was the hard way applied to the institution that was supposed to check it.  When the senate investigated, the senate was punished.  When the ICC investigated, the country withdrew.  When the domestic courts investigated, they produced one conviction and called it progress.

Every door that accountability tried to walk through, the hard way slammed shut.

Except The Hague.  You cannot withdraw from The Hague the way you fire a senator or lock up a witness.  The ICC does not operate within the jurisdiction of the accused.  That is the entire point of its existence.  It was built for the gap between what a country does and what a country will admit to doing.  It was built for the distance between one conviction and thirty thousand dead.

---

I do not know what I felt when I heard the ICC would investigate.  I think I felt the same thing the chief's sergeants felt when we talked about addicts being fixable.  Not belief.  Not yet.  Something closer to permission.  Permission to think that what we saw in that basement was not the end of the story.  Permission to think that the hands reaching through the bars were reaching toward something that would eventually reach back.

There is a theology here and I will not pretend it is not the spine of this book.  Love and justice are not opposed.  The church that teaches mercy without justice is teaching half a gospel.  The court that pursues justice without mercy is building half a system.  The most excellent way holds the paradox.  It always has.  Mercy for the person.  Justice for the act.  The peer-led recovery program that turned surrenderees into counselors — that was love for the broken.  The ICC investigation that named the killing for what it was — that is love for the bereaved.  Both are love.  Both are the most excellent way applied to different wounds.

The mothers in the RISE UP coalition who testified before the ICC are practicing the same courage as the surrenderees who stood in front of their peers and told the truth about what they had done.  Different rooms.  Different bars.  The same act.  Stand up and tell the truth and trust that the truth matters even when the system you are telling it to is slow and imperfect and run by human beings who may fail you.

Duterte said he would rot in jail.  He may.  The trial has not happened yet.  The conviction has not come.  The arc is long and the end is not written.  But the arrest happened.  The man who said the ICC was bullshit was arrested by his own police at his own airport on a warrant from a court he said had no authority over him.

The arc is long.  Everyone who quotes that verse forgets that long means long.  It means decades.  It means the mothers who testified may not live to see the conviction.  It means the men who reached through the bars may never know that the man who put them there faced a judge.  Long means long and justice does not come fast enough to feel like justice while you are waiting for it.

But it comes.

The building in The Hague is glass and steel and clean lines and correct temperature.  It is as far from the basement as architecture can get.  But it is connected to that basement by a line that runs through every document the prosecutor has gathered, every witness statement the mothers have given, every page of evidence that says a government told its police to kill and the police killed and the people died and an alternative existed and was refused.

Duterte is in the dock now.  The man who built the machine sits in a building made of glass in a country that is not his, and the world is doing what docks do.  They do not chase.  They do not knock.  They do not plead.  They hold the accused and let the evidence speak and the witnesses remember and the court decide.

I stood in a basement in Calamba and prayed a prayer that could not be long enough.  The ICC investigation is a longer prayer.  It is the world saying to the dead and the detained and the mothers and the surrenderees and the seventeen-year-old in the alley: we have not forgotten you.  We are slow.  We are imperfect.  We are an institution made of paper and procedure and we cannot bring anyone back.  But we have not forgotten.

That is what love demands.  Not speed.  Not perfection.  Remembering.  The dock remembers.  The witnesses remember.  The court remembers.  The dock is not empty.  Duterte sits in it.

The hands that reached through the bars in that basement were reaching for this too.  They did not know it.  They were reaching for Jesus and for hope and for someone to tell them they were not broken beyond fix.  But they were also reaching for a world where the man who put them behind those bars would one day sit behind a different kind of bar.  Not iron.  Not in a basement.  Glass and steel and clean lines, in a building where the temperature is always correct, in a country where the crying does not have to stop because visitors came downstairs.

The dock is not empty.  Duterte is in it.  Behind bars now, the same as the men he put there.  But he is still reaching through them — not for Jesus, not for the most excellent way, but for the chair.  Still campaigning.  Still planning.  Still grasping for the hard way even from inside the system the hard way built.  Sixty men in six bunkbeds reached through bars toward something better.  Duterte reaches through bars toward the same thing that put everyone else behind them.

Nobody is broken beyond fix.  Not even him.  But he has to reach in the right direction.  And from The Hague, he is still reaching for the chair.

The hands in this book all reach.  The question is what you reach for.

The reckoning happened in glass and steel.  But there is another reckoning happening in language, and that one is closer to home.


# CHAPTER.009 — The Unalived

I had just come home from a celebration of life.  A life well lived, through struggles, the way all lives worth celebrating are.  Pastor Matt MacCollin spoke at the service and he said the thing he always says — write in your bible, because that is what is left behind at the end.  The words you underlined and the margins you filled in are what tell people who you were.

I went home and found my bible.  It was dusty.

Aaron Norris handed me that compact bible when I graduated from New Life Fellowship — a program not unlike the ones we ran for surrenderees in Calamba.  On the first page he wrote: "Andrew I chose this compact bible for you so that you could take it everywhere w/you easily.  You have a tremendous heart for God, don't ever let anyone try to take that from you ever!  Isaiah 55:8.  Love, Aaron Norris."

That bible went with me to the Philippines.  It sat in my bag at the hotel while we descended staircases and knocked on doors and watched hands reach through bars.  And then it sat on a shelf at home while the compartments sealed and the years passed and the dust settled.  The woman in Southblock 7 apologized for the dust on her bible.  I owe the same apology.  We are not so different, she and I.

I wiped it off and opened it and read.  Isaiah 55:8.  "For my thoughts are not your thoughts, neither are your ways my ways, declares the LORD."

His ways are not my ways.  I would not have written this book.  I would not have connected Kraft to Davao to MacCollin to Salem Heights in one night.  I would not have timed the writing to the year Duterte was arrested and Kraft was safely at national headquarters.  I would not have made a love letter to a dictator out of a man counting bunkbeds in horror.  My ways would have left the compartments sealed and the dust on the bible and the story untold.  His ways opened them all on the Friday before Easter.

Now I am going to turn the mirror toward home.  Because his ways are not my ways, and his ways say the story does not end in the Philippines.

---

The word "unalive" causes me more trauma than the thing it is hiding.

I have been in the basement.  I have stood in the middle of cells where sixty men shared six bunkbeds and the crying stopped because visitors walked downstairs.  I have breathed air that was not air but the evaporated sweat of human beings circulated by cheap fans.  I know what happens to people at the end of the hard way.  I know the smell and the heat and the silence after the crying stops.  And I know the language that makes it possible.  Not the violence itself.  The words that come before the violence, the ones that shrink it down small enough to live with.

Tokhang.  The farm.  Nanlaban.  Deterrence.  Enhanced interrogation.  Collateral damage.  Unalive.

Every system that does terrible things to people invents a vocabulary that makes the terrible things sound like paperwork.  In the Philippines, a program named for gentleness — knock and plead — produced mass death.  In America, a platform built to share dance videos taught an entire generation to say "unalive" instead of "kill" because the algorithm would suppress the real word.  And now the real word is disappearing.  Not from the dictionary.  From the mouths of people who should be screaming it.

When you replace the word "kill" with "unalive," you have not protected anyone.  You have made killing smaller.  You have turned it into something that can live on a screen between a recipe video and a cat.  You have done the hard way's work for it, and you have done it for free.

On my thirtieth birthday I was arrested with methamphetamine.  I was walking into a bar ten minutes before two in the morning and an officer patted me down.  He said what's this.  I turned and left.  He tackled me.  The report said I was resisting arrest.  I was begging for a search warrant.  Resisting arrest.  Nanlaban.  He resisted.  The same words, the same mechanism, different country.  I did not learn about the hard way from a research paper.  I learned it on my face on the pavement outside a bar in America on my thirtieth birthday because I asked a police officer for my constitutional rights and that became resistance.  I remember the concrete.  I remember the weight of him on my back.  I remember thinking: this is what it feels like when the system decides you are the problem.  Years later I stood in a basement in Calamba and recognized the architecture.

I am watching my country build the same machinery I watched operate in the Philippines.  The parts are not identical.  The targets are different.  The scale of killing is not comparable.  Not yet.  But the architecture is the same.  The rhetorical infrastructure, the dehumanization pipeline, the religious sanctification of cruelty, the normalization curve — I recognize all of it.  I have been inside the building these blueprints produce.  I have stood in its basement.

---

"These aren't people.  These are animals."  That was 2018.

"Root out the radical left thugs that live like vermin."  That was 2023.  Veterans Day.

"They're poisoning the blood of our country."  That was late 2023, repeated six times into 2024.

"I don't know if you call them people."  That was April 2024.

Rodrigo Duterte, July 2016: "Are they humans?  What is your definition of a human being?"

Read those quotes again.  Remove the names.  Remove the dates.  You cannot tell which country produced them.  The language of dehumanization is not creative.  It does not need to be.  It only needs to do one thing: move a population from "them" to "it."  From people to animals to vermin to something you do not even know if you call people.  The slide is always the same.  The destination is always the same.  And the people at the bottom of that slide are always the poor.

In the Philippines, the drug war killed rickshaw drivers and laborers and teenagers.  Not the drug lords.  The poor.  In America, the hardness falls on the same people it always falls on.  Immigrants working jobs no one else will take.  Communities with no resources.  The unhoused.  The addicted.  The people Jesus called the least of these.  The hard way is always a war on the poor justified by the fears of the comfortable.

I did the privilege math at a mall in the Philippines.  My translator's fiance showed me real estate listings and my American programmer's salary could buy a mansion in a country where dinner is one package of ramen split five ways.  That math works in one direction.  When I fly across the Pacific with dollars in my pocket, I get a real estate tour.  When someone from that same economy crosses a border going the other direction, carrying the same ambition, doing the same math in reverse, they get a detention center.

The difference is not character.  It is not work ethic.  It is not merit.  It is the direction of travel and the color of the passport.

---

In 2019, the Department of Homeland Security's own inspector general found cells designed for thirty-five people holding one hundred and fifty-five.  Children without soap.  A government lawyer argued in court that "safe and sanitary" conditions did not require the government to provide soap.

Cells for thirty-five holding one hundred and fifty-five.  Six bunkbeds holding sixty men.  Same math.  Different country.

I have been in both buildings.  One was a police station basement in Calamba.  The other is a system of detention centers operated for profit by corporations with guaranteed minimum bed counts.  The fans are different.  The paperwork is different.  The bodies pressed together in spaces designed for a fraction of their number are not different at all.

The officers on the six-wheeled vehicles in Calamba wore masks.  They covered their faces because the hard way knows what it is.  You cannot drive down a street pulling fathers out of houses with your face showing.  ICE agents in America wear tactical gear and masks.  They arrive in unmarked vehicles.  In Mississippi in August 2019, they raided poultry plants and detained six hundred and eighty people and children came home from school to empty houses.

The hard way always hides its face.  It has to.  Because the people carrying it out are human beings and human beings cannot do these things with their faces showing.  The mask is not protection from the target.  It is protection from the mirror.

And here is what the most excellent way says to the people behind those masks, in Calamba and in America: we are not coming for you.  We are coming for the system that put you in the mask.  There is a way out.  Your hands can go up too.  The officers in Calamba raised theirs in the barangay meetings because the killing was destroying them and someone offered them something other than more of the same.  The accountability for bad leadership is punishment.  The dock is for the architects.  But being commanded to do terrible things to people needs forgiveness, not a longer sentence.  That is how you de-escalate.  Not amnesty.  Not pretending it did not happen.  Grace for the ones carrying the weight and justice for the ones who loaded it onto them.  An ICE agent carrying out orders he did not write is the same as a Calamba officer driving a six-wheeled vehicle he did not want to drive.  The most excellent way reaches both sides of the bars.

There is a dock.  There are names on lists.  The system has begun extrajudicial killings.  I choose to call it what it is.  I will not soften it.  I will not say "unalive."  I will not say "collateral" or "deterrence" or any other word designed to make you comfortable with what is happening.  People are being killed outside the law by agents of the state and I have been in the building where that road ends.  I have stood in the basement.  I have smelled the fans.  I have heard the crying stop because visitors came downstairs.  If you are waiting for it to get bad enough to call it what it is, I am telling you it is already what it is.  The only question left is what you are going to do about it.

What will you call it?

And before you answer — when we confuse the killing of a CEO with social justice, our hard way is broken too.  The hard way does not become the most excellent way because you point it at someone richer than you.  A fist is a fist regardless of who it is swinging at.  Nobody is broken beyond fix means nobody.  The surrenderee.  The officer.  The dictator.  The CEO.  The moment you put someone outside the reach of love because their title makes them acceptable to hate, you have built your own Tokhang.  You have just renamed it justice.

The hard way has no political party.  It lives on the right and it lives on the left and both fists are fists.

---

The normalization curve is the part that should terrify you.

In 2015, the word "rapists" used to describe immigrants was shocking.  It dominated news cycles.  People called it disqualifying.

In 2018, "these aren't people, these are animals" lasted one news cycle.

In 2023, "vermin" and "poisoning the blood" — language with a specific historical pedigree that I do not need to spell out for you — produced op-eds but no political cost.

In 2024, "I don't know if you call them people" barely registered.

By December 2023, sixty-two percent of Americans supported deporting all undocumented immigrants.  Sixty-two percent.  What was unthinkable became debatable became policy became popular in less time than it takes to pay off a car.

I watched this happen in the Philippines in less than a year.  Duterte took office in June 2016.  By September, the killings had majority support.  The mechanism is the same in both countries.  You say the shocking thing.  The shock fades.  You say the next thing.  The shock fades faster.  Each threshold crossed becomes the new floor, and the next thing that would have been impossible yesterday is now merely controversial today and will be policy tomorrow.  The normalization curve is not a metaphor.  It is a schedule.  I have seen where it ends.  It ends in a basement with sixty men in six bunkbeds and a side room where the crying does not stop unless visitors come downstairs.

---

The church is supposed to be the thing that interrupts this.

In the Philippines, it was not.  The Catholic establishment looked at Be Bold with as much disdain as the addicts did.  We did nothing violently.  Not one harsh word.  We treated everyone with respect and love. Christian nationalists in America are doing the same thing.

The same way Kraft showing us the basement was not a betrayal of his station — it was a man who loved his officers enough to show strangers the truth so something could change.

I am not making a left-right argument.  I am describing a pattern I watched produce mass death in a country I stood in, and I am watching the same pattern assemble itself in the country I live in.  Some of the pastors practicing the hard way may be carrying the same weight Kraft was carrying — looking for an exit, waiting for someone to walk into their office with no binder and tell the truth.  Grace reaches them too.

Robert Jeffress — pastor of a sixteen-thousand-member church in Dallas — cited Romans 13 to argue the government has authority to "do whatever is necessary to secure the border."  The ReAwaken America Tour held events in churches that blended QAnon conspiracy with charismatic worship and authoritarian politics.  Speaker Mike Johnson said "God is the one who raises up those in authority."

The drive between Manila airport and RCGGM is about forty-five minutes.  Forty-five minutes.  On the way in, I watched Manila go back in time in fast forward.  Skyscrapers and fortune and bustle, no different from San Francisco or Portland or Seattle.  Then gradually the glass becomes concrete and the concrete becomes cinder block and the cinder block becomes the jungle and I counted the wires on the power poles because that is what I do.  I have laid cable.  I read infrastructure the way other people read signs.  The number of wires on a pole tells you how connected a place is, how much the world decided it was worth investing in.  Leaving Manila the wires thinned with every mile.  The poles that carried dozens of lines in the city carried fewer and fewer until there were barely enough to keep the lights on.  I was looking at the pipe sizes along the road — water infrastructure, sewage capacity, who gets clean water and who does not.  I was counting the lanes narrowing as we drove south.  I was counting vehicles per traffic light, watching the density drop.  Every measurement told the same story.  The pipes got smaller.  The lanes narrowed.  The lights got further apart.  The country's investment in its own people was readable in the diameter of a pipe.  I was auditing the whole country from a van window the way I would size a server cluster — capacity planning.  Except the resource being planned for was people and the answer south of Manila was fewer.  By the time we reached Calamba I already knew from the infrastructure that the system had decided these people did not count.  In Calauan the count reached zero.  No wires.  The system did not even run a line to the place where Southblock 7 sits, where the woman with the dusty bible makes cards for a living, where dinner is one package of ramen.  The infrastructure's answer for Calauan was not fewer.  It was none.  The bunkbeds were just the final number in a calculation that started at the airport.  Everything I saw on the trip — the holding cells, the roofless houses, the six-wheeled vehicles, the men who could not walk — was forty-five minutes from an international airport.  The first world and the basement share a commute shorter than most Americans drive to work.  James narrated the whole way down.  It was his home turf.  He knew every road and every story on the island.

On the drive home, Leonardo behind the wheel, I watched the highway in reverse.  The cinder block turning back to concrete turning back to glass.  The jungle receding into city.  The same road we had taken in, but I was not the same person who had counted power lines on the way down.  I had been in the basement.  I had seen the hands.  I had stood in the circle.  And now I was riding back through the world that pretended the basement did not exist.

James pointed out a park.  Billy Graham was there once.  His visit was so impactful that people still pointed at the spot decades later.  I looked at that park from the van window and tried to hold two things at the same time — the gospel that had landed there so hard they still talked about it, and the holding cells I had just walked out of in the country that gospel was preached to.  Billy Graham came to the Philippines and preached love.  Decades later his son Franklin blesses strongman politics from a pulpit in America.  The father built something that reached across the Pacific.  The son bent it toward power.  That is the distance the American church has traveled in one generation.  I could see the park from the van and I could feel the distance between what was preached there and what I had just lived through.  The same highway.  The same country.  The gospel and the holding cells sharing an island.

When the church tells a nation that its leader is anointed by God, it has handed that leader something no election can provide: permission that does not expire.  Duterte had the Catholic establishment's silence.  American strongman politics has the evangelical movement's worship band.  Different instruments.  Same song.

The church does not have to build the detention center.  It does not have to staff the raid.  It does not have to separate the family.  It just has to call it righteousness.  It just has to preach Romans 13 without reading the rest of the chapter.  It just has to say God raises up those in authority and leave the implications for the people in tactical gear to work out.  That is how the church becomes the instrument of the hard way.  Not by doing violence but by sanctifying it.

And the people they are sanctifying it against are the same people Jesus spent his entire ministry with.  The poor.  The foreign.  The sick.  The outcast.  The ones nobody else would touch.  Christian nationalism has taken the gospel of a man who was himself a refugee, who was himself arrested by the state, who was himself executed by the empire, and turned it into a rubber stamp for the empire's next operation.

He stayed inside the machine and changed it from the inside and was promoted to one-star general for doing it.  That is the model.  Not escape your church.  Transform it.  Walk into your pastor's office the way we walked into Kraft's conference room.  With no binder.  With the truth.  And watch what happens. The resistance has three fronts.  It had three fronts in the Philippines and it has three fronts here.  The state — accountability through law, through courts, through the slow machinery of justice that eventually put Duterte in the dock.  The streets — the feeding programs and the door-to-door ministries and the people who show up where the hard way has done its work and say nobody is broken beyond fix.  And the church — not by abandoning it but by transforming it from the inside.  The ones who stayed in their pews and spoke up.  The ones who read the whole chapter of Romans 13 and brought it to their pastor rather than finding a different pastor.

I know how this story ends if the church does nothing.  I have been in the building.  I have stood in the basement.  The normalization curve does not plateau on its own.  It has to be interrupted.  And the institution best positioned to interrupt it — the one with the moral authority and the infrastructure and the weekly gathering of millions of people who claim to follow a man who said whatever you do to the least of these you do to me — that institution is currently split between the ones who are interrupting and the ones who are handing out masks.

"Collateral damage" made civilian deaths a line item.  "Tokhang" made mass killing sound like a social visit.

The hard way depends on that flinch.  It depends on people who will not say the word.  It depends on language soft enough to scroll past.  It depends on the distance between the word and the act growing so wide that by the time you see what is actually happening in the cells and the detention centers and the basements, you have already accepted a vocabulary that made it survivable to hear about.

And I am telling you, as someone who stood in the middle of it and breathed the air and heard the silence after the crying stopped: the word is kill.  The word is torture.  The word is death.  Use them.  Because the people inside those buildings cannot afford for you to flinch.

The mirror is not a metaphor.  I held it up and I am showing you what is in it.  You can look or you can look away.  But I have been in the basement, and I am telling you: the building is under construction here.  The blueprints are drawn.  The masks are on.  The language is softening.  The curve is moving.

The question is not whether it can happen here.  It is happening here.

Watching it happen makes me lace up my shoes.  I am getting ready to walk down those stairs.

The book opened going down.  It closes going up.


# CHAPTER.010 — Ascent

The book opened going down a staircase.  It closes going up.

I want to take you back to the text one more time.  First Corinthians thirteen.  You have heard it at weddings.  You have heard it cross-stitched on pillows and printed on coffee mugs.  Love is patient.  Love is kind.  It does not envy.  It does not boast.  You have heard it so many times in soft rooms that it became furniture.

You have been to Calamba now.

Read it again.  Love is patient.  Patient with a police chief who says nobody can fix addicts.  Patient with a system that filled holding cells with sixty men in six bunkbeds.  Patient with a country that learned to talk about mass killing like weather.  Love is kind.  Kind to the surrenderees who were promised help and given cages.  Kind to the officers who raised their hands in barangay meetings because the killing was breaking them too.  Kind to the women in the prison cell who still had their humor and still grabbed Matt's butt because they were not broken.

Love does not dishonor others.  Love does not keep a record of wrongs.  Love always protects, always trusts, always hopes, always perseveres.

Love never fails.

That is not a promise that love prevents suffering.  The men behind those bars suffered.  The woman who used to be a worship leader suffered.  The thirteen-year-old on the sidewalk with gangsters shouting at him from one direction and Jesus being offered from the other suffered.  Love did not stop any of that from happening.  Love never fails means love outlasts the thing that caused it.  The peer-led recovery program is still running.  Duterte is facing the International Criminal Court.  The officers in Calamba who raised their hands are still alive and carrying what they were given in those barangay halls.  Revealing Christ's Glory Global Ministries is still in Calauan.  The program survived the president who made it necessary.  Love never fails is not a greeting card.  It is a field report.

The book has shown you three postures.  I did not plan them.  I did not outline them in a conference room before we flew to Manila.  They revealed themselves in the work.  But looking back across everything that happened in those ten days and everything that has happened since, there are three things the most excellent way does.  Three things it always does, in the Philippines or in your city or wherever you are reading this.

The first is presence.

Your body in the room stops the violence.  Matt prayed in that basement.  He had the mic and he did not skip a beat.  As long as he was praying the crying in the side room stayed stopped.  His prayer was the length of the reprieve.  As long as Pastor Anthony Mariano spoke in a language that sounds beautiful in my memory, we were present.  As long as we were present, the violence was paused.  That prayer could not be long enough.  But it was something.  Five Americans standing in a room they were not supposed to see, and Kraft letting them stay, and the beating paused because witnesses were present.  I was not the one praying.  I was the one standing in horror doing math.  But my body was in the room and that was enough.  Presence is not a strategy.  It is not even faith.  It is a body.  Your body, in the room, where the hard way is doing its work.  The hard way hides.  It wears masks.  It uses euphemisms.  It does not want to be seen.  Show up and it pauses.  That is what presence does.

The second is proclamation.

Nobody is broken beyond fix.  We said it at every door in Southblock 7.  We said it on the stage at Bucal High School.  We said it in the chief's conference room and in the women's prison and in the barangay meetings where officers who had carried out Tokhang raised their hands because they needed to hear it too.  Proclamation is not a sermon.  It is a sentence.  You are not broken beyond fix.  The hard way says you are broken and we will break you further until you comply.  The most excellent way says I was broken and I am not anymore and neither are you.  That sentence, delivered honestly by a person who has lived both sides of it, is the most dangerous thing you can say to a system built on the idea that some people are disposable.

The third is program.

Love builds alternatives that outlast the mission trip.  We flew home after ten days.  The Moral Recovery Program stayed.  A Memorandum of Agreement signed at Eduardo Barretto Sr. Elementary School in Barangay Pansol on November 7, 2018, gave it a permanent home.  What happened after we left — how the program grew, what it became, what the city built from the seed we planted together — that is a story I am still gathering the evidence to tell.  I have heard things.  I have seen references to what Calamba accomplished.  But I will not claim what I have not documented, and that documentation is coming.  What I can tell you is this: the method was transferable.  Surrenderees helping surrenderees.  People closest to the problem addressing the problem.  No binder required.  The program is not the paperwork.  The program is the pattern.  Honesty rather than enforcement.  It worked in Calamba and it is still working and the men who signed that MOA are the proof that love builds things that last longer than the people who started them.

Presence.  Proclamation.  Program.  That is the most excellent way.  Not a theology.  A practice.
