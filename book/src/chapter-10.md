# CHAPTER.010 — Ascent

The book opened going down a staircase.  It closes going up.

I want to take you back to the text one more time.  First Corinthians thirteen.  You have heard it at weddings.  You have heard it cross-stitched on pillows and printed on coffee mugs.  Love is patient.  Love is kind.  It does not envy.  It does not boast.  You have heard it so many times in soft rooms that it became furniture.

You have been to Calamba now.

Read it again.  Love is patient.  Patient with a police chief who says nobody can fix addicts.  Patient with a system that filled holding cells with sixty men in six bunkbeds.  Patient with a country that learned to talk about mass killing like weather.  Love is kind.  Kind to the surrenderees who were promised help and given cages.  Kind to the officers who raised their hands in barangay meetings because the killing was breaking them too.  Kind to the women in the prison cell who still had their humor and still grabbed Matt's butt because they were not broken.

Love does not dishonor others.  Love does not keep a record of wrongs.  Love always protects, always trusts, always hopes, always perseveres.

Love never fails.

That is not a promise that love prevents suffering.  The men behind those bars suffered.  The woman who used to be a worship leader suffered.  The thirteen-year-old on the sidewalk with gangsters shouting at him from one direction and Jesus being offered from the other suffered.  Love did not stop any of that from happening.  Love never fails means love outlasts the thing that caused it.  The peer-led recovery program is still running.  Duterte is facing the International Criminal Court.  The officers in Calamba who raised their hands are still alive and carrying what they were given in those barangay halls.  Revealing Christ's Glory Global Ministries is still in Calauan.  The program survived the president who made it necessary.  Love never fails is not a greeting card.  It is a field report.

---

The book has shown you three postures.  I did not plan them.  I did not outline them in a conference room before we flew to Manila.  They revealed themselves in the work.  But looking back across everything that happened in those ten days and everything that has happened since, there are three things the most excellent way does.  Three things it always does, in the Philippines or in your city or wherever you are reading this.

The first is presence.

Your body in the room stops the violence.  I prayed in that basement because there was nothing else I could do, and as long as I was praying the crying in the side room stayed stopped.  My prayer was the length of the reprieve.  I knew it while I was praying.  I knew that when I said amen and walked back up those stairs, the silence would end.  That prayer could not be long enough.  But it was something.  Five Americans standing in a room they were not supposed to see, and the chief letting them stay, and the beating paused because witnesses were present.  Presence is not a strategy.  It is a body.  Your body, in the room, where the hard way is doing its work.  The hard way hides.  It wears masks.  It uses euphemisms.  It does not want to be seen.  Show up and it pauses.  That is what presence does.

The second is proclamation.

Nobody is broken beyond fix.  We said it at every door in Southblock 7.  We said it on the stage at Bucal High School.  We said it in the chief's conference room and in the women's prison and in the barangay meetings where officers who had carried out Tokhang raised their hands because they needed to hear it too.  Proclamation is not a sermon.  It is a sentence.  You are not broken beyond fix.  The hard way says you are broken and we will break you further until you comply.  The most excellent way says I was broken and I am not anymore and neither are you.  That sentence, delivered honestly by a person who has lived both sides of it, is the most dangerous thing you can say to a system built on the idea that some people are disposable.

The third is program.

Love builds alternatives that outlast the mission trip.  We flew home after ten days.  The Moral Recovery Program stayed.  A Memorandum of Agreement signed at Eduardo Barretto Sr. Elementary School in Barangay Pansol on November 7, 2018, gave it a permanent home.  What happened after we left — how the program grew, what it became, what the city built from the seed we planted together — that is a story I am still gathering the evidence to tell.  I have heard things.  I have seen references to what Calamba accomplished.  But I will not claim what I have not documented, and that documentation is coming.  What I can tell you is this: the method was transferable.  Surrenderees helping surrenderees.  People closest to the problem addressing the problem.  No binder required.  The program is not the paperwork.  The program is the pattern.  Honesty rather than enforcement.  It worked in Calamba and it is still working and the men who signed that MOA are the proof that love builds things that last longer than the people who started them.

Presence.  Proclamation.  Program.  That is the most excellent way.  Not a theology.  A practice.

---

They thought they lacked a system.  They forgot that they themselves were the solution.

That is the sentence that sits underneath everything in this book.  The chief thought he needed a program from Manila.  He had thousands of surrenderees in his city and no framework for what to do with them that did not involve brutality.  He thought he needed a system.  What he got was five people with no binder who told him the truth about themselves.  And from that truth a system grew, but the system was never the point.  The people were the point.  The surrenderees who became the peer leaders.  The officers who raised their hands.  The pastors who signed on.  The RCGGM partners who stayed after we left.  None of them had a binder either.  They had themselves.

I am now saying it to you.

You do not need a government program.  You do not need a ten-step action plan.  You do not need permission from an institution or funding from a foundation or a degree in social work.  You need yourself and you need to be willing to show up and tell the truth.  That is what we brought to Calamba.  That is all we brought to Calamba.  And Calamba built a model that other cities copied.

America is not the Philippines.  I know that.  The scale of killing is not the same.  The machinery is different.  But the rhetorical infrastructure is the same.  The dehumanization is the same.  They're not people.  They're animals.  They're poisoning the blood.  I have heard that language before.  I heard it in a country where it ended in holding cells and cardboard signs on bodies in the street.  I watched a democracy learn to cheer for death and I am watching mine learn the same vocabulary.  The normalization curve is shorter than anyone wants to believe.

And the church.  The church that should be the first to stand up and say nobody is broken beyond fix is instead blessing the hand that dehumanizes.  The Catholics in the Philippines looked at us with disdain for loving the people they had written off.  Christian nationalists in America are doing the same thing.  Different target, same mechanism.  Call a population invaders or criminals or parasites, wrap it in scripture, and let the state do the rest.  The church does not have to pull the trigger.  It just has to bless the hand that does.

But there are churches that have not bent.  The Sanctuary Movement sheltered people in their buildings when the government came for them.  Reverend William Barber called Christian nationalism what it is: theological malpractice.  Shane Claiborne's The Simple Way in Philadelphia has been doing the same work Be Bold does, presence in neighborhoods the system has abandoned.  Catholic Charities and Lutheran Immigration and Refugee Service resettle more refugees than any government agency.  There are hope dealers everywhere.  You do not have to start from nothing.  You can join what already exists.

Or you can start from nothing.  We did.

---

Keith gashed his shin stepping off a dock near the end of the trip.  It was bad.  The kind of wound that in a clean American emergency room would get stitches and a follow-up appointment and a week off your feet.  Keith did not have a week.  We were finishing in the Philippines and flying straight to India.  He called it the enemy trying to stop us.  I do not know if I would have used those words.  But I know what I saw.  A man with his shin laid open who wrapped it and limped through the rest of the Philippines and then got on a plane and limped through India.  That is what a hope dealer does.  Bleeds and keeps going.  Does not wait for the wound to close before walking into the next room where people need to hear that nobody is broken beyond fix.

I think about that a lot.  Not because Keith is superhuman.  Because the work does not wait for you to be ready.  It does not wait for your shin to heal or your funding to come through or your theology to be airtight or your life to be in order.  The hill in Southblock 7 does not wait for an American to come back and climb it.  Someone is climbing it right now.  Leonardo Fabella, who told me to stop, is still there.  Pastor Anthony Mariano is still there.  RCGGM is still there.  The path keeps going up after you turn back.

---

Nobody is broken beyond fix.

Not even the officer who tackled me on my thirtieth birthday outside a bar for asking for a search warrant.  I have forgiven him.  Not because what he did was right.  Because he was part of a broken system and broken systems break the people inside them.  He was somebody's nanlaban — a man doing what the system trained him to do, writing "resisting arrest" in a report the same way officers in Calamba wrote nanlaban in theirs.  If I cannot forgive him, I cannot stand in a barangay meeting and tell the officers along the walls that their hands can go up too.  If the most excellent way does not reach the man who put me on the pavement, then it does not work.  It works.  I am the proof.  He is the test.  And I pass it — not because I am good, but because Jesus is and he told me to.

Including the reader.  Including whoever is holding this book right now and thinking they are too far gone or too comfortable or too complicit or too tired.  Including the officers in Calamba who raised their hands.  Including the woman in Southblock 7 who went inside and came back with a bible she had not thrown away.  Including the ones who were holding the gun.  The most excellent way does not draw a line and say everyone on this side is redeemable and everyone on that side is not.  The chief of police in Calamba ran holding cells where men were beaten in side rooms and he sat at a table and told five strangers he would take whatever they had because he wanted a way out.  Love reached him too.  That is the hardest part.  Not loving the victims.  Loving the ones who are inside the system and looking for an exit.

Including America.  This country is not broken beyond fix.  The hard way is loud right now.  It is popular right now.  It has the microphone and the pulpit and the applause.  But the most excellent way has never operated by volume.  It operates at the door.  One knock, one conversation, one person remembering something they thought they had lost.  A woman holding a bible.  A chief laughing because someone told him he could exhale.  A teenager choosing Jesus while the gangsters shout.

The book you just read showed you all the wrong escapes.  The gaming cafe in Southblock 7 where kids stole to disappear into screens.  The bottle that kept a father from bringing home a package of ramen.  The farm where surrenderees were sent to vanish.  Sanitized language that made killing small enough to ignore.  A church that blessed the hard way instead of opposing it.  Every one of those was an escape.  None of them worked.  The title of this book is not a warning.  It is a direction.  Escaping the hard way.  There is one escape that works and you have watched it work for ten chapters.  The most excellent way.  Honesty rather than enforcement.  Presence rather than avoidance.  Love that builds things that outlast the people who started them.

That is the door.  It is open.  Don't escape alone.

---

I stood on that path in Southblock 7 and looked up the hill.  The building materials along the sides.  The jungle closing in.  The assistant pastor behind me telling me I could not go further.  I could see the path continuing.  There were people up there who needed to hear what we had been saying at every door below.  Nobody is broken beyond fix.  I wanted to climb.  I was told to stop.  I stopped.

But the path did not stop.

It kept going up through the green, past the building materials and the half-finished walls, past the place where my limit met wisdom, past everything I could see into everything I could not.  Someone is on that path right now.  Someone who lives there.  Someone who knows the hill the way the assistant pastor knows it, the way I know the streets back home.  Someone is climbing.

The work is not finished.  The hill does not end.  The hands are still reaching through the bars.

Go up.

In case you have not guessed it yet, the most excellent way is love.  Agape.  The kind that does not ask what you have done.  The kind that reaches through bars.

And the doors open.
