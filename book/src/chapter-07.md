# CHAPTER.007 — Hands

The kid was maybe thirteen.  It was night.  We were walking the streets near RCGGM in Calamba, doing what we did — talking to anyone who would stop, telling them about Jesus.  The kid stopped.  He listened.  He had that look kids get when they are hearing something for the first time that matches something they already suspected.  He was small and serious and he accepted Christ right there on the sidewalk.

Two teenagers sat outside a building maybe thirty feet away.  They started shouting at him.  Come back.  Get over here.  They were not his friends in the way that matters.  They were gangsters, or close enough.  The kind of older boys who own a younger boy's schedule and call it loyalty.  They were not asking.

The kid stood between us.  The entire war for a human soul was happening on a residential sidewalk in the Philippines at nine o'clock at night.  On one side, two men in Hope Dealer shirts saying nobody is broken beyond fix.  On the other, two teenagers who had already decided what this kid was going to become.  The most excellent way pulling one direction.  The hard way pulling the other.  A child in the middle who had just said yes to Jesus and now had to walk home past the boys who heard him say it.

I do not know what happened to that kid.  I have thought about him more than almost anyone else in this book.  I do not know if he went back to the teenagers.  I do not know if the prayer held or if the sidewalk swallowed it.  I do not know his name.  I know his face and I know he was reaching for something and I know two other sets of hands were reaching for him at the same time.

That is the image I cannot put down.  Hands.  Everyone in this story is reaching.

The women's prison was not like the men's holding cells.  It was smaller.  Ten to twenty women, roughly as many beds.  Still a cage, still bars, still the thick stale heat of a Philippine detention facility.  But the bodies were not stacked three deep.  There was room to breathe in there.  Whether that made it better or worse is a question I am not qualified to answer.

We stood outside the cell with a loudspeaker.  The bars between us and them.  We shared testimonies from the other side, the same brutal honesty we brought everywhere — addiction, violence, what Jesus did with the wreckage.  But here I had to go further than I had gone anywhere else.

I told them about my wife.

My drinking became blacking out and my blacking out became abusive.  At first I tried to deny it.  Then I fought it, calling my wife a liar.  But bruises and scars do not lie.  When she offered me a choice between her and drinking, it was too easy to drink.  That is what addiction does.  It takes the person you are supposed to love more than anything and makes them lighter than a bottle.  I chose the bottle.  She left.  The separation lasted years.

I stood on the wrong side of the bars and told them that through a loudspeaker.  Every word of it.  The drinking, the blackouts, the abuse I denied, the wife I called a liar, the bruises I pretended were not there, the choice I made wrong.  I told women in a cage the kind of man I had been and some of those women were likely in that cage because of men exactly like the one I was describing.  I told them Jesus washed it clean — not erased it, not pretended it did not happen, but took the filth of what I had done and made something new out of what was left.  I told them my wife came back.

I do not know what their faces did because I could not look up for most of it.  Telling that story at Bucal High School cost me something.  Telling it to the students at the recovery programs cost me something.  But telling it to women in a prison cell — women who had been on the receiving end of men like the one I used to be — through a loudspeaker, through a translator, through bars — cost me everything I had.  The translator was a woman.  She carried my confession from English to Tagalog without flinching.  I do not know what that cost her either.

Something shifted.  Not in a dramatic way.  Not a collapse or a shout.  Something in the air between the bars changed.  The women listened and the distance closed.  I was not a visitor anymore.  I was a man who had done what men do to women, standing outside their cage admitting it, saying there is a way out of being that person.  The most excellent way is not a program for good people.  It is a program for people like me.  I was the evidence.

They let us in.

That is not something you earn quickly.  You do not walk up to a cage full of women and get invited inside.  You earn it by breaking your own heart in front of them first.  We broke our hearts through a loudspeaker and through bars and they watched us do it and then whoever made the decision — the guards, the women themselves, I do not know — decided we could come inside.

One of the women grabbed Matt's butt.

I am putting that in this book because it happened and because it matters.  We had just been let inside after pouring out the worst things about ourselves and then immediately the room was light with laughter because a woman in a Philippine prison cell decided that Matt looked grabbable and she went for it.  Matt turned red.  The women laughed.  We laughed.  The guards probably laughed.  That moment did more for the humanity in that cell than anything I said into the loudspeaker.  These women were not tragic.  They were not broken creatures waiting to be saved by Americans with microphones.  They were people — people who flirted, people who laughed, people who had been locked up long enough to know that if you cannot find something funny inside a cage you will not survive it.  The grab said: we are still here.  We are still us.  Do not mistake this cell for the end of us.

We locked arms for a photo.  Hope dealers and inmates, standing together inside the cell.  This was the moment — the one we had earned through the loudspeaker and the confession and the breaking of hearts through bars.  In that photo the bars disappear.  You cannot tell who is the prisoner and who is the visitor.  Four men who had been on the wrong side of every law they encountered, arms linked with women who were currently on the wrong side.  The distance between us was nothing.  The distance had always been nothing.  The bars were the lie.

You will not see that photo.  We were not allowed to share it.  Being let into the cell was the worst thing the guards could do and the photo was the evidence they did it.  So the image exists only in the memory of the people who were in that room — the women, the guards, and five Americans who earned their way through the bars by breaking themselves open first.  You will have to imagine it.  Arms linked.  Smiling.  The cage around all of us and none of us belonging in it.

There were other cells we spoke to that day.  Other women, other faces.  The details blur.  Memory does that — it saves what burned the brightest and lets the rest go soft.  I remember the photo.  I remember Matt's face.  I remember the weight of my own words coming back at me in Tagalog from a woman who had to hold them in her mouth long enough to translate them.  The rest I carry as a feeling, not a film.

I remember Josh looking for wifi after we left the prison.  He was always looking for wifi.  Carla was back home and he needed to check in, to tell her we were OK, to relive some of what we had just experienced with the person who knew him best.  Josh learned to eat peanut butter oatmeal with sugar during a kitchen assignment at OSP.  That is still the way he eats it.  A man who discovered his comfort food behind bars in Oregon was now standing in Philippine holding cells telling the men inside that there is a way out.  Josh did not talk as much as Matt or me.  He did not need to.  He carried his proof quietly — in the way he checked on Carla through borrowed wifi, in the way he stood in rooms full of suffering and did not flinch, in the way a man who has been inside a cage moves differently around cages than a man who has not.  The women in those cells probably saw it in him before he said a word.

The athletes were the lightest scene in the trip and maybe the most important.

Kids playing ball on a concrete basketball court under a covered pavilion with low walls open to the air.  Sixty or seventy kids packed onto the court — the littlest ones no more than five or six, the oldest in basketball jerseys, all of them there because somebody organized a game and promised food afterward.  A speaker sat on the edge of the court for what came next.  A sports ministry session — one of the programs Be Bold supported.  The idea was simple and it was old and it worked.  A kid in Calamba has three options after school.  Go home to the house where dad came back drunk and dinner is not happening.  Go to the corner where the older boys are recruiting.  Or go to the field where someone organized a game and there are Jollibee hamburgers afterward.

The hamburger is not incidental.  The hamburger is the strategy.  You cannot feed a kid's soul if his stomach is empty.  You cannot talk to a kid about Jesus if he is thinking about food.  The Jollibee hamburger opens the door.  The game opens it wider.  And then, when the door is open and the kid is fed and tired and happy from playing, someone picks up a microphone on the stage and tells the truth about their life.

The same pattern.  Always the same pattern.  Food, then presence, then honesty.  The feeding in Southblock 7.  The conference room with the chief.  The loudspeaker outside the women's cell.  You show up.  You bring whatever you have.  You earn the right to speak by being there first.  The most excellent way does not lead with a sermon.  It leads with a hamburger.

The kids on that field did not know a ministry moment was coming when they showed up to play ball.  They came for the game.  They stayed for the food.  And somewhere between the game and the food, a door opened that nobody announced, and honesty walked through it.  That is how the most excellent way works.  It does not advertise.  It does not recruit.  It shows up as something you need and by the time you realize what it actually is, you have already let it in.

I watched those kids eat Jollibee hamburgers and I thought about the thirteen-year-old on the sidewalk.  This field was the alternative.  This was the place that kid should have been instead of on a dark street being shouted at by gangsters.  Every hamburger on that field was a hand reaching for a kid before the street could reach him first.

I have been writing about hands for seven chapters and did not realize it until now.

The men in the holding cell in Chapter 1, three rows deep, every one of them reaching through the bars.  The ones on the beds who could not walk, reaching anyway.  The woman in Southblock 7 who came to the door holding her bible like she had been waiting for someone to knock.  The officers in the barangay meetings who raised their hands during the call — police officers, the ones carrying out the hard way, reaching for the same thing the surrenderees were reaching for.  The chief of police who ducked his head into a room and stopped the crying — that was a hand reaching too, the only way he knew how.  The mayor's wife who had been reaching toward her husband for years before we arrived.  The kid on the sidewalk reaching for Jesus while the gangsters reached for him.  The women reaching through bars, reaching for a laugh, reaching for the photo.  Matt reaching for his dignity after the grab.

Hands everywhere.  All of them reaching for the same thing.

A way out.

An escape from the hard way.

The hard way is a fist.  It closes around people and it does not let go.  Tokhang was a closed fist.  The farm was a closed fist.  The six-wheeled vehicles driving the streets at night were closed fists.  The system that says addicts cannot be fixed, that the poor are disposable, that strength means bodies — that is a fist, and it has been closed around communities like Southblock 7 and holding cells like the one beneath the Calamba police station for as long as men with power have decided that other men are not worth saving.

The most excellent way is an open hand.

That is all it is.  An open hand reaching toward a closed fist and saying: you can let go.  Nobody is broken beyond fix.  Not the men behind the bars.  Not the women in the cell.  Not the kid on the sidewalk.  Not the chief who wanted to stop the crying.  Not the officers who raised their hands in the barangay meetings because the killing was destroying them too.  Not the gangsters shouting at a thirteen-year-old to come back.  Not even them.

The whole book is hands.  I did not plan it that way.  I just kept writing about what I saw, and what I saw, every time, in every room and on every street and in every cell, was people reaching.  Reaching for hope.  Reaching for someone who would not quit on them.  Reaching for proof that the hard way was not the only way.

Some of those hands I held.  Some I could not reach.  Some I held for a moment and then the airplane brought me home and I do not know if they are still reaching or if someone else took hold.  The kid on the sidewalk.  The women behind the bars.  The men three rows deep.

I think about their hands.  I think about them all the time.

Those hands were reaching for something beyond us.  They were reaching, though they did not know it yet, for a reckoning.
