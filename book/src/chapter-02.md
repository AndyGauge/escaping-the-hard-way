# CHAPTER.002 — Knock

The word sounds gentle if you do not know what it means.  Tokhang.  Bisayan.  *Toktok* — knock.  *Hangyo* — plead.  Knock and plead.  It sounds like something a neighbor does when they need to borrow rice.  It sounds like mercy.  That was the point.  You name a thing gently when you need people to accept it before they understand what it is.

Rodrigo Duterte was inaugurated on June 30, 2016.  Tokhang launched the next day.  Not a week later.  Not after a transition period or a policy review.  The next day.  The speed tells you it was never a policy.  It was a promise being collected.  Duterte had campaigned on killing drug users and drug dealers and the people who voted for him did so knowing exactly what they were voting for.  He told them during the campaign that he would fill Manila Bay with bodies.  They elected him by six million votes.

The program worked like this.  Barangay officials — neighborhood captains, essentially — compiled watch lists.  Names of suspected drug users and dealers in their jurisdiction.  The lists were supposed to come from intelligence.  They came from rumors.  They came from grudges.  They came from neighbors who owed money or wanted a property or had an argument about a rooster.  They came from barangay captains who were paid to add a name or remove one.  The lists were as accurate as the people who wrote them, and the people who wrote them were human beings with debts and jealousies and scores to settle.  Your name on a list was not evidence.  It was a sentence.  I remember the men in the holding cells below the Calamba station and I think about those lists now.  Every one of those sixty men started as a name on a piece of paper compiled by someone who may or may not have known them.

If your name was on the list, you had two options.  Surrender or wait.

The ones who waited got the knock.

Between ten at night and four in the morning, convoys entered the neighborhoods.  Six-wheeled armored vehicles, V-150 transports, rolling through streets built for tricycles and pedestrians.  The officers on those vehicles wore masks.  Balaclavas.  Ski masks.  The kind of covering that exists for one reason, which is to allow the person beneath it to do something they cannot do with their face showing.  They cordoned streets.  They kicked in doors.  They executed operations that the reports would later describe with a single word: *nanlaban*.  He resisted.

Nanlaban.  It appeared in police report after police report like a stamp.  He resisted, so we killed him.  She resisted, so we killed her.  The seventeen-year-old resisted, so we killed him.  The consistency was its own confession.  Every suspect resisted.  Every encounter required lethal force.  Every body found with a drop gun beside it, a .38 revolver placed in the hand of someone who had been breathing ten minutes earlier.  Drugs materialized beside the dead.  Sachets of shabu appeared on kitchen tables in houses that had been torn apart.  The evidence arrived after the killing because the killing was the point and the evidence was the paperwork.

Duterte said it out loud.  That was the thing about him.  He did not bother with the pretense.  "If you know of any addicts, go ahead and kill them yourself," he said at his inauguration.  Not in a closed meeting.  Not in a leaked memo.  At his inauguration, in public, to the nation.  "I'd be happy to slaughter them," he said, comparing himself to Hitler and getting the Holocaust body count wrong in the same sentence.  "I will protect you," he told police.  "If you kill a thousand persons, I will protect you."  He told officers to plant evidence on suspects, then when journalists reported it he called it a joke.  Everything was a joke.  The killing was serious and the language around it was a joke and the country had to decide which one to believe, and sixty-five million voters had already made that decision.

The bodies appeared in the streets and in the alleys and along the rivers.  They appeared with cardboard signs placed on their chests or tied around their necks.  *Pusher Ako, Huwag Tularan.*  I am a pusher.  Do not emulate.  The signs were written in marker on torn cardboard, the handwriting of someone who wanted you to read it quickly and understand it completely.  The bodies were a message and the signs were the translation.  This is what happens.  This will happen to you.  The cardboard was the cheapest material available for a public service announcement and it was brutally effective.

The official count from the Philippine National Police acknowledged six thousand two hundred to six thousand six hundred killed in operations.  Human Rights Watch and Amnesty International estimated between twelve thousand and thirty thousand dead when you included the vigilante killings that the police did not claim and did not investigate.  The gap between those numbers is a field of bodies that nobody in Manila wanted to count.  Twelve thousand is the floor.  Thirty thousand is the ceiling.  Somewhere between those numbers is the truth, and the truth was lying in the streets with cardboard on its chest.

In August 2017, officers killed a seventeen-year-old boy named Kian delos Santos.  The police report said he was a drug courier.  The police report said nanlaban.  He resisted.  But Kian was killed in an alley with CCTV cameras, and the cameras showed something different from the report.  They showed officers dragging a teenager through the dark.  They showed him on his knees.  They showed what nanlaban actually looked like when someone was watching.  The footage went public.  The country saw it.  Three officers were eventually convicted in November 2018, the same month I was in Calamba looking at the product of the system that killed him.  Three officers held accountable out of a machine that had produced twelve thousand bodies.  Three convictions in two and a half years.  The math of accountability was worse than the math of the holding cells.

But the dead were not the largest number.  The largest number was the living.

By 2018, between 1.2 and 1.8 million people had surrendered.  They walked into police stations and barangay halls and signed their names on forms and raised their hands and said yes, I am on the list, I am what you say I am, I surrender.  They did this because the alternative was a knock at two in the morning and a cardboard sign.  They surrendered because surrender sounded like it came with a program.  The government promised rehabilitation.  Counseling.  Livelihood support.  Medical assistance.  The Community-Based Drug Rehabilitation Program.  CBDRP.  It had an acronym, which meant it was real.

What the surrenderees actually got was Zumba.

I need you to sit with that.  A million people turned themselves in to the state, put their names on a government list, and the rehabilitation program they were promised was group Zumba sessions and community prayer.  That was the program.  That was the infrastructure the Philippine government built to address the addiction crisis it had declared a national emergency.  You surrendered.  You signed the form.  Your name went on a list that made you more visible to the same officers driving the six-wheeled vehicles.  And then someone taught you an aerobics routine and told you to pray about it.

The list was the cruelest part.  Before you surrendered, your name might have been on a watch list compiled by a barangay captain with a grudge.  After you surrendered, your name was on a government list compiled by the state itself.  You had confirmed what the watch list only alleged.  You had given the machinery your address, your face, your confession.  If the Zumba did not fix you — and I don't need to tell you whether Zumba fixes methamphetamine addiction — you were now a documented failure of a government program, which made you a justified target for the next operation.  Surrender made you safer for a week and more vulnerable forever.

The prisons could not hold them.  The Philippines already had among the most overcrowded prison systems on the planet before Duterte took office.  Manila City Jail operated at five hundred percent capacity.  Five times the number of human beings the building was designed to contain.  Sleeping in shifts.  Families paying guards to bring food and bedding.  The cells I saw in Calamba were not an aberration.  They were the national standard.  Six bunkbeds and sixty men was the system working as designed, because the system was never designed to hold people.  It was designed to process them.  In through the door, name on the list, body in a cell, out to the farm.

The farm.  The chief in Calamba said the word without saying what it meant.  He said it the way you say the name of a place you have sent people to that you do not want to describe.  The farm was what came after the holding cells.  It was where the surrenderees went when the cells were full and the Zumba had not fixed them and Manila needed the numbers to move.  Nobody used the word farm to mean a farm.  It was a word that covered whatever happened next, and whatever happened next was not something the chief wanted to talk about in detail.  The farm was the end of the sentence that started with a knock on a door at two in the morning.  Tokhang to nanlaban to the holding cell to the farm.  Knock, plead, resist, disappear.

I arrived in Calamba on November 1, 2018.  Two and a half years into this.  By then the drug war was not a crisis.  It was the weather.  It was the thing that happened and continued happening and people lived around it the way you live around monsoon season.  The six-wheeled vehicles drove the streets and the masks were on the officers and the cardboard signs had stopped being shocking and started being scenery.  Twelve thousand dead or twenty thousand dead or thirty thousand dead and the country had absorbed it.  The polls showed majority support.  The president's approval ratings stayed above seventy percent.  A democracy had voted for mass killing and then approved of the result and the word for that is normalization and normalization is the thing that should terrify you more than the killing itself, because killing can be stopped but a country that has learned to accept killing has to unlearn it, and unlearning is slower and harder and nobody has written the program for that either.

That is what we walked into.  Five Americans from Salem Heights with no binder and no program manual.  Into a system that had produced a million surrenderees and given them Zumba.  Into a police station where the chief had been told to be strong and strong meant bodies.  Into holding cells where the men could not walk because of what had been done to them.  Into a country that had normalized the knock the way you normalize a commute.

The chief sat at the head of that table because he had the authority to kill and the authority meant nothing because it did not solve the problem.  He could fill the cells and empty them to the farm and fill them again and the drugs would still be there and the addicts would still be there and the neighborhoods would still be broken.  The hard way gave him a cycle, not a solution.  And the only alternative Manila had given him was a dance class.

When five Americans who used to be exactly where his surrenderees were walked into his conference room and told him the truth about themselves, he did not evaluate our credentials.  He did not ask for our methodology.  He said we'll take whatever you have, and he laughed, because for two and a half years the only thing anyone had brought him was a body count and a Zumba instructor.

We brought whatever we had.  It was not much.  It was ourselves.

He took us downstairs.  You already know what we found.

Tokhang.  Knock and plead.  The gentlest word for the hardest thing.  A whole country knocked on, a whole country pleaded with, and the only answer anyone had given was more knocking.  Harder.  Louder.  With masks on and guns drawn between ten at night and four in the morning.

We knocked too.  We knocked on the chief's door and on the doors in Southblock 7 and on the bars of the holding cells.  But our knock was the original meaning of the word.  The one before Duterte took it and made it into something that required a mask.  We knocked the way a neighbor knocks.  We knocked the way you knock when you need to borrow something, except what we were offering to lend was the proof that the men on the other side of the door were not beyond repair.

The word still means what it means.  *Toktok.*  Knock.  Somebody has to reclaim it.

And somebody had to answer the door.  In Calauan, they did.
