# CHAPTER.009 — The Unalived

I had just come home from a celebration of life.  A life well lived, through struggles, the way all lives worth celebrating are.  Pastor Matt MacCollin spoke at the service and he said the thing he always says — write in your bible, because that is what is left behind at the end.  The words you underlined and the margins you filled in are what tell people who you were.

I went home and found my bible.  It was dusty.

Aaron Norris handed me that compact bible when I graduated from New Life Fellowship — a program not unlike the ones we ran for surrenderees in Calamba.  On the first page he wrote: "Andrew I chose this compact bible for you so that you could take it everywhere w/you easily.  You have a tremendous heart for God, don't ever let anyone try to take that from you ever!  Isaiah 55:8.  Love, Aaron Norris."

That bible went with me to the Philippines.  It sat in my bag at the hotel while we descended staircases and knocked on doors and watched hands reach through bars.  And then it sat on a shelf at home while the compartments sealed and the years passed and the dust settled.  The woman in Southblock 7 apologized for the dust on her bible.  I owe the same apology.  We are not so different, she and I.

I wiped it off and opened it and read.  Isaiah 55:8.  "For my thoughts are not your thoughts, neither are your ways my ways, declares the LORD."

His ways are not my ways.  I would not have written this book.  I would not have connected Kraft to Davao to MacCollin to Salem Heights in one night.  I would not have timed the writing to the year Duterte was arrested and Kraft was safely at national headquarters.  I would not have made a love letter to a dictator out of a man counting bunkbeds in horror.  My ways would have left the compartments sealed and the dust on the bible and the story untold.  His ways opened them all on the Friday before Easter.

Now I am going to turn the mirror toward home.  Because his ways are not my ways, and his ways say the story does not end in the Philippines.

---

The word "unalive" causes me more trauma than the thing it is hiding.

I have been in the basement.  I have stood in the middle of cells where sixty men shared six bunkbeds and the crying stopped because visitors walked downstairs.  I have breathed air that was not air but the evaporated sweat of human beings circulated by cheap fans.  I know what happens to people at the end of the hard way.  I know the smell and the heat and the silence after the crying stops.  And I know the language that makes it possible.  Not the violence itself.  The words that come before the violence, the ones that shrink it down small enough to live with.

Tokhang.  The farm.  Nanlaban.  Deterrence.  Enhanced interrogation.  Collateral damage.  Unalive.

Every system that does terrible things to people invents a vocabulary that makes the terrible things sound like paperwork.  In the Philippines, a program named for gentleness — knock and plead — produced mass death.  In America, a platform built to share dance videos taught an entire generation to say "unalive" instead of "kill" because the algorithm would suppress the real word.  And now the real word is disappearing.  Not from the dictionary.  From the mouths of people who should be screaming it.

When you replace the word "kill" with "unalive," you have not protected anyone.  You have made killing smaller.  You have turned it into something that can live on a screen between a recipe video and a cat.  You have done the hard way's work for it, and you have done it for free.

On my thirtieth birthday I was arrested with methamphetamine.  I was walking into a bar ten minutes before two in the morning and an officer patted me down.  He said what's this.  I turned and left.  He tackled me.  The report said I was resisting arrest.  I was begging for a search warrant.  Resisting arrest.  Nanlaban.  He resisted.  The same words, the same mechanism, different country.  I did not learn about the hard way from a research paper.  I learned it on my face on the pavement outside a bar in America on my thirtieth birthday because I asked a police officer for my constitutional rights and that became resistance.  I remember the concrete.  I remember the weight of him on my back.  I remember thinking: this is what it feels like when the system decides you are the problem.  Years later I stood in a basement in Calamba and recognized the architecture.

I am watching my country build the same machinery I watched operate in the Philippines.  The parts are not identical.  The targets are different.  The scale of killing is not comparable.  Not yet.  But the architecture is the same.  The rhetorical infrastructure, the dehumanization pipeline, the religious sanctification of cruelty, the normalization curve — I recognize all of it.  I have been inside the building these blueprints produce.  I have stood in its basement.

---

"These aren't people.  These are animals."  That was 2018.

"Root out the radical left thugs that live like vermin."  That was 2023.  Veterans Day.

"They're poisoning the blood of our country."  That was late 2023, repeated six times into 2024.

"I don't know if you call them people."  That was April 2024.

Rodrigo Duterte, July 2016: "Are they humans?  What is your definition of a human being?"

Read those quotes again.  Remove the names.  Remove the dates.  You cannot tell which country produced them.  The language of dehumanization is not creative.  It does not need to be.  It only needs to do one thing: move a population from "them" to "it."  From people to animals to vermin to something you do not even know if you call people.  The slide is always the same.  The destination is always the same.  And the people at the bottom of that slide are always the poor.

In the Philippines, the drug war killed rickshaw drivers and laborers and teenagers.  Not the drug lords.  The poor.  In America, the hardness falls on the same people it always falls on.  Immigrants working jobs no one else will take.  Communities with no resources.  The unhoused.  The addicted.  The people Jesus called the least of these.  The hard way is always a war on the poor justified by the fears of the comfortable.

I did the privilege math at a mall in the Philippines.  My translator's fiance showed me real estate listings and my American programmer's salary could buy a mansion in a country where dinner is one package of ramen split five ways.  That math works in one direction.  When I fly across the Pacific with dollars in my pocket, I get a real estate tour.  When someone from that same economy crosses a border going the other direction, carrying the same ambition, doing the same math in reverse, they get a detention center.

The difference is not character.  It is not work ethic.  It is not merit.  It is the direction of travel and the color of the passport.

---

In 2019, the Department of Homeland Security's own inspector general found cells designed for thirty-five people holding one hundred and fifty-five.  Children without soap.  A government lawyer argued in court that "safe and sanitary" conditions did not require the government to provide soap.

Cells for thirty-five holding one hundred and fifty-five.  Six bunkbeds holding sixty men.  Same math.  Different country.

I have been in both buildings.  One was a police station basement in Calamba.  The other is a system of detention centers operated for profit by corporations with guaranteed minimum bed counts.  The fans are different.  The paperwork is different.  The bodies pressed together in spaces designed for a fraction of their number are not different at all.

The officers on the six-wheeled vehicles in Calamba wore masks.  They covered their faces because the hard way knows what it is.  You cannot drive down a street pulling fathers out of houses with your face showing.  ICE agents in America wear tactical gear and masks.  They arrive in unmarked vehicles.  In Mississippi in August 2019, they raided poultry plants and detained six hundred and eighty people and children came home from school to empty houses.

The hard way always hides its face.  It has to.  Because the people carrying it out are human beings and human beings cannot do these things with their faces showing.  The mask is not protection from the target.  It is protection from the mirror.

And here is what the most excellent way says to the people behind those masks, in Calamba and in America: we are not coming for you.  We are coming for the system that put you in the mask.  There is a way out.  Your hands can go up too.  The officers in Calamba raised theirs in the barangay meetings because the killing was destroying them and someone offered them something other than more of the same.  The accountability for bad leadership is punishment.  The dock is for the architects.  But being commanded to do terrible things to people needs forgiveness, not a longer sentence.  That is how you de-escalate.  Not amnesty.  Not pretending it did not happen.  Grace for the ones carrying the weight and justice for the ones who loaded it onto them.  An ICE agent carrying out orders he did not write is the same as a Calamba officer driving a six-wheeled vehicle he did not want to drive.  The most excellent way reaches both sides of the bars.

There is a dock.  There are names on lists.  The system has begun extrajudicial killings.  I choose to call it what it is.  I will not soften it.  I will not say "unalive."  I will not say "collateral" or "deterrence" or any other word designed to make you comfortable with what is happening.  People are being killed outside the law by agents of the state and I have been in the building where that road ends.  I have stood in the basement.  I have smelled the fans.  I have heard the crying stop because visitors came downstairs.  If you are waiting for it to get bad enough to call it what it is, I am telling you it is already what it is.  The only question left is what you are going to do about it.

What will you call it?

And before you answer — when we confuse the killing of a CEO with social justice, our hard way is broken too.  The hard way does not become the most excellent way because you point it at someone richer than you.  A fist is a fist regardless of who it is swinging at.  Nobody is broken beyond fix means nobody.  The surrenderee.  The officer.  The dictator.  The CEO.  The moment you put someone outside the reach of agape because their title makes them acceptable to hate, you have built your own Tokhang.  You have just renamed it justice.

The hard way has no political party.  It lives on the right and it lives on the left and both fists are fists.

---

The normalization curve is the part that should terrify you.

In 2015, the word "rapists" used to describe immigrants was shocking.  It dominated news cycles.  People called it disqualifying.

In 2018, "these aren't people, these are animals" lasted one news cycle.

In 2023, "vermin" and "poisoning the blood" — language with a specific historical pedigree that I do not need to spell out for you — produced op-eds but no political cost.

In 2024, "I don't know if you call them people" barely registered.

By December 2023, sixty-two percent of Americans supported deporting all undocumented immigrants.  Sixty-two percent.  What was unthinkable became debatable became policy became popular in less time than it takes to pay off a car.

I watched this happen in the Philippines in less than a year.  Duterte took office in June 2016.  By September, the killings had majority support.  The mechanism is the same in both countries.  You say the shocking thing.  The shock fades.  You say the next thing.  The shock fades faster.  Each threshold crossed becomes the new floor, and the next thing that would have been impossible yesterday is now merely controversial today and will be policy tomorrow.  The normalization curve is not a metaphor.  It is a schedule.  I have seen where it ends.  It ends in a basement with sixty men in six bunkbeds and a side room where the crying does not stop unless visitors come downstairs.

---

The church is supposed to be the thing that interrupts this.

In the Philippines, it was not.  The Catholic establishment looked at Be Bold with as much disdain as the addicts did.  We did nothing violently.  Not one harsh word.  We treated everyone with respect and love.  And the dominant church in the country — the institution that should have been the first to stand between the state and the powerless — had made its peace with the hard way.  Our presence was an indictment they did not ask for.  Loving the people they had written off exposed the fact that they had written them off.  The church did not pull the trigger.  It just blessed the hand that did.

Christian nationalists in America are doing the same thing.

I need you to hear what I am about to say and what I am not saying.  I am not demonizing the church.  This book is dedicated to people who have been demonized and I will not do it to anyone, including pastors.  Calling out sin inside the church is biblical.  Having hard conversations with leaders who cling to the hard way is what scripture demands.  The prophets did it.  Jesus did it in the temple with a whip.  Paul did it in his letters.  Naming the hard way inside the church is not an attack on the church.  It is love for the church.  The same way Kraft showing us the basement was not a betrayal of his station — it was a man who loved his officers enough to show strangers the truth so something could change.

I am not making a left-right argument.  I am describing a pattern I watched produce mass death in a country I stood in, and I am watching the same pattern assemble itself in the country I live in.  Some of the pastors practicing the hard way may be carrying the same weight Kraft was carrying — looking for an exit, waiting for someone to walk into their office with no binder and tell the truth.  Grace reaches them too.

Michael Flynn stood in a church and said America needs to have one religion.  Robert Jeffress — pastor of a sixteen-thousand-member church in Dallas — cited Romans 13 to argue the government has authority to "do whatever is necessary to secure the border."  The ReAwaken America Tour held events in churches that blended QAnon conspiracy with charismatic worship and authoritarian politics.  Speaker Mike Johnson said "God is the one who raises up those in authority."

I remember passing a park in the Philippines and being told Billy Graham was there once.  His visit was so impactful that people still pointed at the spot decades later.  Billy Graham preached the gospel in a park in the Philippines and people remembered.  His son Franklin now blesses strongman politics from a pulpit in America.  The father built something that reached across the Pacific.  The son bent it toward power.  That is the distance the American church has traveled in one generation.  From a park where the gospel landed so hard they still talk about it, to a platform where the gospel is used to justify the hard way.  I stood near that park.  I felt the echo of what the father had done.  The distance between that echo and what the son is doing now is the distance the church has fallen.

When the church tells a nation that its leader is anointed by God, it has handed that leader something no election can provide: permission that does not expire.  Duterte had the Catholic establishment's silence.  American strongman politics has the evangelical movement's worship band.  Different instruments.  Same song.

The church does not have to build the detention center.  It does not have to staff the raid.  It does not have to separate the family.  It just has to call it righteousness.  It just has to preach Romans 13 without reading the rest of the chapter.  It just has to say God raises up those in authority and leave the implications for the people in tactical gear to work out.  That is how the church becomes the instrument of the hard way.  Not by doing violence but by sanctifying it.

And the people they are sanctifying it against are the same people Jesus spent his entire ministry with.  The poor.  The foreign.  The sick.  The outcast.  The ones nobody else would touch.  Christian nationalism has taken the gospel of a man who was himself a refugee, who was himself arrested by the state, who was himself executed by the empire, and turned it into a rubber stamp for the empire's next operation.

---

But the church is not one thing.  It never has been.

In the Philippines, the Catholic establishment made its peace with the hard way.  But Revealing Christ's Glory Global Ministries stayed in Calauan knocking on doors.  Leonardo Fabella told me to stop climbing the hill and then kept climbing it himself after I flew home.  The pattern of resistance was there even when the dominant institution failed.

It is here too.

Hundreds of congregations revived the Sanctuary Movement in 2017, offering physical shelter to people marked for deportation.  Reverend William Barber and the Poor People's Campaign called Christian nationalism what it is: theological malpractice.  Thousands of clergy signed the Christians Against Christian Nationalism declaration.  Catholic Charities and Lutheran Immigration and Refugee Service remain the largest refugee resettlement providers in the country — churches doing the work that other churches are preaching against.  Shane Claiborne's The Simple Way in Philadelphia operates the same way Be Bold does in Salem Heights, the same way RCGGM operates in Calauan.  Different streets.  Same pattern.  Same most excellent way.

But I am not telling you to leave your church.  Too many people leave looking for the right group rather than leading the group where God put them.  Kraft did not transfer out of the Philippine National Police.  He did not resign in protest.  He did not go find a nicer institution.  He stayed inside the machine and changed it from the inside and was promoted to one-star general for doing it.  That is the model.  Not escape your church.  Transform it.  Walk into your pastor's office the way we walked into Kraft's conference room.  With no binder.  With the truth.  And watch what happens.  Your pastor might be Kraft — a man carrying something he wants to put down, waiting for someone honest enough to give him permission.

The resistance has three fronts.  It had three fronts in the Philippines and it has three fronts here.  The state — accountability through law, through courts, through the slow machinery of justice that eventually put Duterte in the dock.  The streets — the feeding programs and the door-to-door ministries and the people who show up where the hard way has done its work and say nobody is broken beyond fix.  And the church — not by abandoning it but by transforming it from the inside.  The ones who stayed in their pews and spoke up.  The ones who read the whole chapter of Romans 13 and brought it to their pastor rather than finding a different pastor.

I know how this story ends if the church does nothing.  I have been in the building.  I have stood in the basement.  The normalization curve does not plateau on its own.  It has to be interrupted.  And the institution best positioned to interrupt it — the one with the moral authority and the infrastructure and the weekly gathering of millions of people who claim to follow a man who said whatever you do to the least of these you do to me — that institution is currently split between the ones who are interrupting and the ones who are handing out masks.

---

I will not call killing "unaliving."  I said that in the author's note and I mean it here.  The sanitization of language is not a minor complaint.  It is the leading edge of the normalization curve.  Every euphemism is a down payment on the next atrocity.  "Enhanced interrogation" made torture possible.  "Deterrence" made family separation policy.  "Collateral damage" made civilian deaths a line item.  "Tokhang" made mass killing sound like a social visit.

And "unalive" is training a generation to flinch from the word that describes what their government is doing to people.

The hard way depends on that flinch.  It depends on people who will not say the word.  It depends on language soft enough to scroll past.  It depends on the distance between the word and the act growing so wide that by the time you see what is actually happening in the cells and the detention centers and the basements, you have already accepted a vocabulary that made it survivable to hear about.

I will not participate in that.  I have been in the room.  I know what the language is hiding.  And I am telling you, as someone who stood in the middle of it and breathed the air and heard the silence after the crying stopped: the word is kill.  The word is torture.  The word is death.  Use them.  Because the people inside those buildings cannot afford for you to flinch.

Nobody is broken beyond fix.  I said it at every door in Southblock 7 and I say it about America.  But the fix requires you to see clearly what is broken.  And you cannot see clearly through a vocabulary designed to blur your vision.

The mirror is not a metaphor.  I held it up and I am showing you what is in it.  You can look or you can look away.  But I have been in the basement, and I am telling you: the building is under construction here.  The blueprints are drawn.  The masks are on.  The language is softening.  The curve is moving.

The question is not whether it can happen here.  It is happening here.

Watching it happen makes me lace up my shoes.  I am getting ready to walk down those stairs.
