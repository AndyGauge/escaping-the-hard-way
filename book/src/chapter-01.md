# CHAPTER.001 — Descent

The conference room had the setup of a negotiation.  Senior Superintendent Kirby John Kraft, Acting Provincial Director of the Laguna Provincial Police Office, sat at the head of the table with his men flanking each side, sergeants or whatever rank they held.  They wore the posture of men accustomed to being in charge of the room.  The chief did all the talking.  His men watched us the way you watch something you haven't decided is a threat or a waste of time.

On our side of the table sat five hope dealers from Salem Heights in black polos and BBSM hats.  Be Bold Street Ministries, The Most Excellent Way.  Matt, Josh, Keith, James, and me.  James had put the whole thing together.  He and Keith were the straight edge guys, accountants who knew how to build a bridge between institutions.  The other three of us were the broken ones.  We had flown across the Pacific with no binder, no program manual, no official presentation.  We had ourselves.  The chief needed something for the surrenderees.  Thousands of people had turned themselves in under Tokhang, Duterte's knock and plead program, and now they were a logistics problem.  You cannot shoot all of them.  You cannot feed all of them.  You cannot put them back on the streets because Manila is watching the numbers.  The surrenderees needed to go to a program but the programs were unstructured.  The chief needed a solution and he had run out of ideas that didn't involve brutality.

He explained the problem like a man explaining why a machine is broken.  The surrenderees were addicts.  Addicts cannot be fixed.  The holding cells were overcrowded.  The farm was the only option he had been given and the farm was not something he wanted to talk about in detail.  Nobody could fix addicts, he told us.  That was the wall.  That was the end of every conversation he had with Manila, with the mayor, with his own men.  Nobody could fix addicts.

Three addicts, fixed, and two men who had never touched the stuff but showed up anyway, stared blankly back at him.

I don't think any of us said it in that moment.  We didn't need to announce it like a reveal on a television show.  It wasn't a strategy.  It was just the truth sitting on our side of the table.  Three of us were living proof that the most excellent way worked.  We had been the ones on the list.  We had been the ones people gave up on.  Keith and James had never touched the stuff, but they showed up anyway because the most excellent way is not only for the broken — it is also for the ones who stand beside them.  The difference between us and the men in his holding cells was that somebody had not quit on us.

The chief was not what I expected.  I expected a man who enjoyed the hard way.  I expected the face of the machine.  What I got was a man carrying a weight he did not ask for.  Manila told him to be strong and strong meant bodies.  His officers were executing operations that broke them in ways they would not talk about for years.  He sat at the head of that table because he had all the power in the room, but power is not the same thing as control.  He controlled nothing.  He was being driven by a mandate from the president that treated human lives like arithmetic.  Reduce the number.  Any method is acceptable.  Report the results.

We presented ourselves.  That was all we had.

Matt opened.  He looked at the chief of police in a country where being a drug addict could get you killed and said, "I heard you were killing drug addicts, so I thought you ought to know that I'm the worst one."

The room did not know what to do with that.  I don't think the chief had ever had someone walk into his station and volunteer for the list.  Josh and I were quick to add ourselves to make sure they didn't take Matt away.  If one of us was on the list, all of us were on the list.  That was the deal.  Three Americans surrendering themselves to the hard way in a conference room in Calamba.  We were not there to observe from a safe distance.  We were the people the hard way was designed for, sitting across from it, daring it to take us.  We told the chief of police in Calamba that we used to hurt people for fun.  That our depravity was so deep we did not recognize it as depravity while we were in it.  We told him about dealing.  Not the sanitized version, not the version you tell a church on a Sunday morning.  We told him about the power.  What it feels like to have a neighborhood depend on you, to have people afraid of you, to control who gets well and who gets sick.  We told him about the money and the respect and the way everyone moves when you walk into a room.

Then we told him about losing it.  The crushing lows of homelessness.  Sleeping in places a person should not have to sleep.  Watching your body fall apart.  Watching the people who feared you step over you.  The distance between the top of the drug trade and the bottom of the street is shorter than anyone on either end believes.

We told him how many people we had met since then and helped.  Not with programs.  Not with frameworks.  With our own hands and our own stories.  People we sat with while they detoxed.  People we drove to meetings.  People we picked up off the same streets we had been picked up from.  We had no binder to hand him.  We just came with brutal honesty.  The pitch was five people who had been exactly where his surrenderees were, sitting upright and breathing and looking him in the eye.

We told him about The Most Excellent Way, about what it looked like to walk alongside someone through recovery instead of dragging them to a cell.  We told him surrenderees could help other surrenderees.  That the people closest to the problem were the ones most capable of addressing it.  That the answer to addiction was not a bullet or a cage but a community of people who had been there and come out the other side.

At some point we told him we were trying to find the support they needed.  He looked at his men, looked back at us, and said, "We will take whatever you have."

The room laughed.  It was the first honest sound anyone had made since we sat down.  His sergeants laughed.  We laughed.  It was the laugh of a man who has been holding his breath for months and someone finally told him he could exhale.  Whatever you have.  Not a negotiation.  Not a counter-proposal.  A man at the end of his options saying yes before he even knew what he was saying yes to.

That laugh changed the room.  The negotiation setup collapsed.  The us and them dissolved into something that was not quite trust but was close enough to work with.  He wasn't evaluating our proposal anymore.  He was deciding whether we were real.

I think that's why he took us downstairs.

It was not on the schedule.  Nobody had planned for the chief of police to walk five Americans into the basement of his station.  But something in the way we talked about the surrenderees, the way we talked about them as people who could recover instead of problems to be processed, something in that cracked a door he did not have to open.

The staircase went down one flight.  The chief walked ahead of us like a man showing you a wound he's been hiding.

On the way down, Kraft ducked his head into a side room.  He said something I could not hear, something short, the tone of a man issuing a correction he wished he did not have to make.  The crying stopped.

I had not realized there was crying until it stopped.  The absence was louder than the sound had been.  Whatever was happening in that room, it had been happening long enough that my ears had incorporated it into the background noise of the descent.  The chief told them to stop and they stopped and the silence that replaced it pressed against every surface of that staircase.

Then the smell hit.  The air changed before the light did.  It got heavier, wetter, sour with the smell of too many bodies in not enough space.

The cells were on both sides of us.  In front and behind.  We stood in the middle of it, not looking in from the outside but surrounded.  Bars to the left, bars to the right, men pressed against them in every direction.  Six bunkbeds.  I counted them because counting was something to do with my eyes that was not looking at the men.  Six bunkbeds and at least sixty men.  They were on the beds, under the beds, along the walls, sitting with their knees drawn up because there was not enough floor to stretch out.  The math of it doesn't make sense until you see it.  You think you understand overcrowding and then you stand in a room where ten men share a space designed for one and you realize the word overcrowding was invented by people who had never been in this room.

I remember the fans.  Lots of fans.  They were everywhere in that room, spinning and pushing air that had no business being called air across the bodies of sixty men.  The fans kept everyone evaporating their sweat and that is what you breathed.  The moisture of sixty men being circulated by cheap electric fans bolted to whatever surface would hold them.  The heat did not go away.  The fans just moved it around and gave it texture.

Kraft did not need to let us in there.  He did not need to show us the worst thing happening under his authority.  He could have kept us in the conference room, shaken our hands, and sent us back to our hotel.  Instead he took us down the staircase and showed us the truth.  That was courage.  A man inside a brutal system chose to show strangers the thing he could not show anyone else because nobody else had walked into his station and talked about addicts like they were people.  He was not showing us the system's success.  He was showing us his burden.  He was asking for help the only way a man in his position could.

Matt prayed.  He had the mic.  Our first stop on the ministry tour and it was a cage and Matt did not skip a beat.  He opened his mouth and prayed over sixty men in six bunkbeds the way he did everything else on this trip — without hesitation, without performance, without the pause that would have told the room he was afraid.  Maybe he was afraid.  He did not show it.

I did not pray.  I stood there.  I have been in jail enough times to know how to put on a good face and I put one on.  But standing outside that cage was less comfortable than being inside one ever was in Oregon.  Inside a cage you know the rules.  Outside a cage watching sixty men reach through bars while your friend prays over them is a different kind of horror.  I was reliving the scene in my head while it was still happening.  My body was in the room.  My mind was trying to leave.

As long as Matt was praying, we were present.  As long as we were present, whatever was happening in that side room was paused.  His prayer was the length of the reprieve.  I knew it while he was praying.  I knew that when he said amen and we walked back up those stairs, the silence would end.

That prayer could not be long enough.  And it was not mine.  It was Matt's.

While he prayed I could not stop thinking about how many of them we were the last choice they got.  That the farm was their next stop.  That tomorrow another set of faces would be staring back from behind those bars and the men reaching through them right now would be gone.  This was not a prison where you serve a sentence and come out.  This was a holding cell.  The word holding does not mean kept.  It means waiting.  They were waiting to be moved and where they were moving was not somewhere you come back from.  We were the last voice some of them would hear that said they were worth saving.  And I was doing math.  Counting bunkbeds.  Counting men.  Counting how many would be gone tomorrow and how many new faces would replace them.  Compartmentalizing the horror the same way bureaucracy does — turning people into numbers because numbers are survivable and faces are not.  That is what the mind does when it cannot process what the eyes are seeing.  Manila ran Tokhang on math.  Reduce the number.  Report the results.  I was running on the same math standing in its basement.  Not because I was the system.  Because math was the only thing between me and the floor.

Few people were prepared to see what was below those stairs.  I do not think I was one of them.  Above us, six-wheeled vehicles drove up and down the streets of Calamba.  The officers on those vehicles wore masks.  The men carrying out the hard way covered their faces.  The state gave them the authority and the masks gave them the permission.  You cannot do what they were doing with your face showing because the hard way knows what it is.  It hides.  Behind masks, behind language, behind euphemisms like the farm and words like tokhang.  The machinery of the hard way doing its work.  Below us, the product of that machinery sat in cages.  Men who had surrendered because the alternative was a body on the pavement with a cardboard sign.  They had raised their hands to the state and the state had put them here.

We made the call.  The call to Jesus.  For many of them it was their last chance to surrender to anything that was not going to destroy them.  They had surrendered to Duterte's program and it gave them six bunkbeds and sixty bodies and a room where the crying doesn't stop unless visitors come downstairs.  This was a different surrender.  This was not hands up because a six-wheeled vehicle is outside your house.  This was hands up because someone is offering you something other than the hard way.

I did not see one person not reaching through those bars with their hands up when the call happened.

Not one.

The hands came from in front of us and behind us.  From both sides.  Three rows deep, men pressed against each other to get their hands through the bars.  The ones in the front were crushed against the iron by the weight of the men behind them.  The second row reached over shoulders and between heads.  The third row, the ones farthest from us, the ones who could not even see us, pressed their bodies into the men in front of them to push their arms through.  Three rows of human beings compressing themselves against each other and against the bars in every direction because all of them wanted to reach toward something.  The ones on the beds, the ones who could not walk because of what had been done to them, they reached too.  They could not stand.  They could not press themselves into the rows at the bars.  They lay where the torture had left them and stretched their arms out from the beds toward the sound of the call.  Sixty men in a basement with the fans spinning and the sweat evaporating off their skin and every single one of them stretching through iron.  Hands up.  All of them.  Men who had nothing left, who had been told by their own government that they were not worth fixing, three rows deep pressing against each other to reach four strangers who had just told them they used to be exactly where they are.

I have been in rooms where the call goes out and half the people shift in their seats.  I have been in churches where a handful respond.  I have been on streets where one person out of twenty will stop and listen.  I have never been in a room where every single person reached.  The bars did not stop them.  They pushed their arms through and raised their hands and in that moment the holding cell in the basement of a police station in Calamba became the most honest church I have ever stood in.

That is what I remember.  Not exactly what I said to God.  I remember the hands.  I remember the heat and the smell and the sound of sixty men responding to the only offer anyone had made them that did not involve a cell or a farm or a bullet.  I remember that Kraft watched it happen and did not say a word.  I think he was seeing for the first time what he had been hoping was possible.

The chief walked us back up.  Nobody spoke.  There is a particular silence that follows when a group of people has seen something together that none of them were prepared for.  It is not the silence of having nothing to say.  It is the silence of having too much to say and no words that fit.

We walked out of the station into the sun.  The Philippines does not care what you just saw.  The heat and the light hit you the same way they did before you went down those stairs.  The jeepneys are still loud.  The street vendors are still selling.  The world continued while sixty men sat in six bunkbeds and someone in a side room was told to stop.

Five hope dealers stood in the parking lot of a police station in Calamba and did not say anything to each other for what felt like a long time.  Five addicts, fixed, who had just seen what happens to the ones nobody fixes.

That is why we went to Southblock 7.  That is why we went door to door in Calauan telling anyone who would listen that nobody is broken beyond fix.  That is why we led the recovery programs that gave the surrenderees somewhere to go that was not the cells and not the farm.  Because we had been in the room.  We had seen the alternative.  The hard way has a smell and a sound and a silence, and once you have experienced it you either go home and forget or you go to the streets and fight it with the only thing that has ever worked.

We did not speak one harsh word in their country.  We did not raise a hand.  We did not condemn the chief or his men or the system that built those holding cells.  We treated everyone with respect and love because that is the most excellent way and because five of us were living proof that it works.

Nobody can fix addicts.

We were sitting right there.

The smell of that basement followed us out into the sun.  It followed us to the streets.  It is following me still.
