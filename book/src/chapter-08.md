# CHAPTER.008 — The Dock

The International Criminal Court sits in The Hague, in the Netherlands, in a building designed to look like justice.  Glass and steel and clean lines.  It is the kind of building where the temperature is always correct.  The air does not smell like sixty men.  There are no fans bolted to the walls.  There is no crying in the side room.  It is as far from the basement of a police station in Calamba as architecture can get.

Rodrigo Duterte called it bullshit.

He said he would rot in jail before he answered to a foreign court.  He said the ICC was useless.  He said what every strongman says when the machinery of accountability starts moving in his direction: that it is imperialism, that it is politics, that the people who are coming for him do not understand what he was dealing with.  The drug problem was real.  The threat was real.  He saved the country.  That is the script.  It has not changed since Nuremberg.  The details shift but the defense is always the same.  I had no choice.  They made me do it.  The ones I killed deserved it.

The ones he killed were the men behind the bars I prayed over.  The ones in the side room where the crying stopped.  The surrenderees in the holding cells and the bodies on the pavement with cardboard signs and the seventeen-year-old named Kian delos Santos who was dragged into an alley by three police officers and shot.  Kian was seventeen.  The officers said he fought back.  The security camera said he did not.  In November 2022, while I was processing what I had seen in Calamba exactly four years earlier, three officers were convicted of killing him.  It was the only domestic conviction to come out of the entire drug war.  One boy.  Three officers.  Thousands of dead.  One conviction.

That is what domestic accountability looked like in the Philippines.  One case, out of what human rights organizations estimate at twelve thousand to thirty thousand killings.  One.

The ICC exists for the gap between one and thirty thousand.

---

The timeline moves the way justice moves.  Slowly.  In February 2018, the ICC prosecutor opened a preliminary examination into the killings.  The following month, Duterte pulled the Philippines out of the Rome Statute.  The withdrawal was theater.  It took a year to take effect and the court retained jurisdiction over everything that happened between November 2011 and March 2019.  The Davao Death Squad.  Tokhang.  The holding cells.  The farm.  All of it still within the court's reach because you cannot withdraw from a treaty and take your crimes with you.

In September 2021, the Pre-Trial Chamber authorized a formal investigation.  The charge was crimes against humanity.  Article 7.  Murder.  Not isolated incidents.  Not rogue officers.  A widespread and systematic attack on a civilian population pursuant to state policy.  The language is precise because international law has to be precise when it is accusing a president of orchestrating mass killing.  Widespread means it was not an accident.  Systematic means it was not a mistake.  State policy means it came from the top.

Two months later the investigation was suspended.  The Philippines claimed it was handling things domestically.  The court waited.  In January 2023 the ICC rejected the deferral.  The domestic proceedings were insufficient.  Of course they were.  You do not ask the machine to investigate itself and expect it to find fault in its own engineering.  The investigation resumed.

By July 2023, Prosecutor Karim Khan confirmed active evidence-gathering.  Witnesses testified from outside the Philippines because testifying from inside meant dying.  The RISE UP coalition — mothers and widows of the dead — cooperated with the investigation at a cost I cannot calculate from the safety of my desk.  These women buried their sons and husbands and then walked into rooms and said his name and what was done to him and who did it.  They knew their name was on a list now.  And they knew how their government managed lists of names.  The surrenderees had put their names on a list and it made them more vulnerable, not less.  These mothers did the same thing — surrendered their anonymity to the ICC — knowing exactly what surrender had cost the last million people who tried it.  I remember the hands in the basement reaching through the bars.  These mothers reached too, through a different kind of bar — the bar of witness testimony, of legal proceedings, of saying the truth to a foreign court while their own country tried to silence them.

On March 11, 2025, Rodrigo Duterte landed at Ninoy Aquino International Airport after attending a political rally in Hong Kong.  He was still campaigning.  Still active.  Still the man the people allegedly wanted.  The Philippine National Police and Interpol arrested him at the airport in an operation called Pursuit.  He was held at Villamor Air Base and transferred to The Hague within twenty-four hours.  His daughter Sara said he was forced onto the plane.  The man who told his police to kill a thousand people and promised to protect them was the first leader from Asia to face trial before the International Criminal Court.  The dock is not empty anymore.

---

But the dock is not the point.  The dock is where the story ends.  The point is that the story does not end.

Argentina's Dirty War killed thirty thousand people.  It took more than thirty years for the convictions to come.  The Khmer Rouge emptied Cambodia's cities and filled its mass graves and the tribunal did not deliver its first conviction until 2010, more than three decades after the killing fields.  In Rwanda, Felicien Kabuga financed a genocide in 1994 and was not arrested until 2020.  Twenty-six years.  Radovan Karadzic hid for thirteen years after the Bosnian War.  Ratko Mladic hid for sixteen.  Both were found.  Both were convicted.

The pattern is not speed.  The pattern is inevitability.  Once proceedings begin, almost no one permanently escapes.  The timeline stretches.  The fugitives age.  The governments change.  But the court does not forget and the witnesses do not stop testifying and the evidence does not expire.  Justice is patient in the way that only institutions without elections can be patient.  No one votes on whether to continue pursuing crimes against humanity.  The mandate outlasts the news cycle.

The pattern held.  Thirty years for Argentina.  Thirty for Cambodia.  Twenty-six for Rwanda.  Nine for Duterte.  The strongmen hide and the courts wait and the witnesses keep testifying and eventually the dock is not empty anymore.  March 11, 2025.  An airport.  Handcuffs.  The man who told his police to kill a thousand people and promised to protect them was arrested by police who were no longer protecting him.  He awaits his mayoral candidacy if he is ever released.  The people, allegedly, still want him.  The hard way does not end when the handcuffs close.  A democracy that learned to cheer for death does not unlearn it because a court in The Hague issues a warrant.  The dock can hold the man.  It cannot hold the appetite that put him in power.  That appetite is still there, in the Philippines and in America, waiting for the next person who promises to be strong.  His daughter Sara is the vice president.  His son is starting right where he left off, ensuring the power balance never changes.  The hard way is a family business.  You arrest the father and the son sits down in the same chair and the machine does not even pause.  The dock can hold Duterte.  It cannot hold the dynasty.  It cannot hold the idea that strength means bodies and the poor are disposable and the right last name is a license to continue.  That is not a problem a court can solve.  That is a problem only the most excellent way can solve, one barangay meeting at a time, one hand raised at a time, one officer at a time choosing a different way.  The court removes the man.  The people have to remove the appetite.

But here is something I did not know until after I started writing this book.  The man who held the Hope Dealer shirt in Calamba, the man who took us to the basement, the man who laughed and said he would take whatever we had — Kirby John Kraft was transferred to Davao.  He became the City Director of the Davao City Police Office.  The birthplace of the hard way.  The city where the death squads started.  The city Duterte plans to return to as mayor when he is released.  And the most excellent way got there first.  The man who watched sixty men reach through bars in his own holding cell was running the police in Duterte's hometown before the dynasty could reclaim it.  I did not plan that.  Kraft did not plan that.  But the God who sent five knuckleheads from Salem Heights to a conference room in Laguna is the same God who sent the man in that conference room to Davao.  The most excellent way does not stay where you plant it.  It follows the hard way to its source.

Kraft spent three years in Davao.  Three years is not a rotation.  It is a tenure.  Long enough to change the culture, train the officers, embed the approach.  Whatever the most excellent way looked like in Laguna, he had three years to plant it in the city where the hard way was born.  And then he was promoted.  Not fired.  Not forced out.  Promoted.  To Metro Manila.  Then Mindanao.  Then national headquarters at Camp Crame, where he now manages the personnel and records of every police officer in the Philippines.  A one-star general.  The man who said he would take whatever we had climbed from a provincial office to the desk that decides where every officer in the country goes and what they do when they get there.

The most excellent way did not stay in a conference room in Laguna.  It followed Kraft to Davao, where it had three years to work before the dynasty tries to return.  The officers he trained are still there.  The culture he built does not leave when he does.  The contagion climbed the ranks.  It is still climbing.

---

I was in Calamba in November 2018.  The same month the officers who killed Kian delos Santos went to trial.  I did not know it at the time.  I was in the basement and on the streets and in the school and at the doors in Southblock 7 and I did not know that somewhere in Manila a courtroom was doing the only thing a courtroom in the Philippines would do about thirty thousand dead.  One case.  One boy.  While I was watching sixty men reach through bars, a country was putting exactly three officers on trial and calling it accountability.

I think about the chief.  The man who sat at the head of the conference table and said he would take whatever we had.  The man who walked us into the basement and showed us the cells and told the crying to stop.  Where does he fall?  He is not Duterte.  He is not the architect.  He is not the man who went on television and told police officers to kill.  But he ran the station.  He managed the cells.  He sent his officers out in the six-wheeled vehicles with masks on their faces.  He carried the weight of what Manila demanded and he carried it all the way down to that basement.

I know what Kraft did with what he was given.  He showed us the cells when he did not have to.  He laughed when we offered ourselves.  He watched sixty men raise their hands to a call he could not make himself, and I saw in his eyes that he was watching something he had been praying for.  He was carrying the weight of a system he did not build, and when five strangers walked in and offered him a different way, he took it.  He took it and he carried it from Laguna to Davao to national headquarters.  That is not complicity.  That is a man who chose the most excellent way from inside the machine and spent the next seven years proving it worked at every level they sent him to.

But here is where I must be careful with my own heart.  The officers who raised their hands in the barangay meetings are part of the same system the ICC is investigating.  The men who drove the six-wheeled vehicles and wore the masks are the same men who stood along the walls and reached for Jesus when the call went out.  If I celebrate their hands raised in one chapter and call for their prosecution in another, I have abandoned the most excellent way.  Grace has to reach them too.  Justice and grace are not enemies.  The dock exists for the architects, for the ones who built the machine and ordered it to kill.  But the officers who operated it — the ones the chief said were being destroyed by what they were asked to do — they were reaching for a way out.  I saw their hands go up.  I will not pretend I did not see that.  The most excellent way does not pick who gets grace.  The men behind the bars got it.  The women in the prison got it.  The chief got it.  The officers get it too.  Justice for the system.  Grace for the people trapped inside it.  That is the paradox and I will not resolve it because it is not mine to resolve.  It is God's.

The ICC investigation covers the period from November 2011 to March 2019.  The chief was operating within that window.  Whether investigators have looked at Calamba, whether the chief's name appears in any file in The Hague, I do not know.  What I know is that the Calamba Moral Recovery Program — the program that grew from our visit, the program that sent surrenderees to help other surrenderees, the program that won awards — proves something the prosecution needs proved.  An alternative existed.  A humane alternative.  It was available.  It worked.  People recovered.  People who were supposed to be unfixable got fixed.  And the killing continued anyway.

That is the moral case.  Not just that people died, but that they did not have to.  The hard way was a choice.  Every body on the pavement with a cardboard sign was a choice made by a government that had another option and refused it.  The Calamba Model is evidence not in the legal sense but in the human sense.  It is the proof that the most excellent way was sitting right there at the table, offering itself, and the machine chose the bullet instead.

---

Senator Leila de Lima tried to stop it from the inside.  She led Senate hearings on the killings.  She brought witnesses.  She named names.  In February 2017, she was arrested on drug charges that anyone paying attention understood were political.  She spent nearly seven years in detention.  Seven years.  A senator.  Locked up for doing the thing senators are supposed to do, which is hold power accountable.  The message was not subtle.  Investigate the killings and you become one of the prisoners.  De Lima's detention was the hard way applied to the institution that was supposed to check it.  When the senate investigated, the senate was punished.  When the ICC investigated, the country withdrew.  When the domestic courts investigated, they produced one conviction and called it progress.

Every door that accountability tried to walk through, the hard way slammed shut.

Except The Hague.  You cannot withdraw from The Hague the way you fire a senator or lock up a witness.  The ICC does not operate within the jurisdiction of the accused.  That is the entire point of its existence.  It was built for the gap between what a country does and what a country will admit to doing.  It was built for the distance between one conviction and thirty thousand dead.

---

I do not know what I felt when I heard the ICC would investigate.  I think I felt the same thing the chief's sergeants felt when we talked about addicts being fixable.  Not belief.  Not yet.  Something closer to permission.  Permission to think that what we saw in that basement was not the end of the story.  Permission to think that the hands reaching through the bars were reaching toward something that would eventually reach back.

There is a theology here and I will not pretend it is not the spine of this book.  Love and justice are not opposed.  The church that teaches mercy without justice is teaching half a gospel.  The court that pursues justice without mercy is building half a system.  The most excellent way holds the paradox.  It always has.  Mercy for the person.  Justice for the act.  The peer-led recovery program that turned surrenderees into counselors — that was love for the broken.  The ICC investigation that named the killing for what it was — that is love for the bereaved.  Both are love.  Both are the most excellent way applied to different wounds.

The mothers in the RISE UP coalition who testified before the ICC are practicing the same courage as the surrenderees who stood in front of their peers and told the truth about what they had done.  Different rooms.  Different bars.  The same act.  Stand up and tell the truth and trust that the truth matters even when the system you are telling it to is slow and imperfect and run by human beings who may fail you.

Duterte said he would rot in jail.  He may.  The trial has not happened yet.  The conviction has not come.  The arc is long and the end is not written.  But the arrest happened.  The man who said the ICC was bullshit was arrested by his own police at his own airport on a warrant from a court he said had no authority over him.

The arc is long.  Everyone who quotes that verse forgets that long means long.  It means decades.  It means the mothers who testified may not live to see the conviction.  It means the men who reached through the bars may never know that the man who put them there faced a judge.  Long means long and justice does not come fast enough to feel like justice while you are waiting for it.

But it comes.

The building in The Hague is glass and steel and clean lines and correct temperature.  It is as far from the basement as architecture can get.  But it is connected to that basement by a line that runs through every document the prosecutor has gathered, every witness statement the mothers have given, every page of evidence that says a government told its police to kill and the police killed and the people died and an alternative existed and was refused.

Duterte is in the dock now.  The man who built the machine sits in a building made of glass in a country that is not his, and the world is doing what docks do.  They do not chase.  They do not knock.  They do not plead.  They hold the accused and let the evidence speak and the witnesses remember and the court decide.

I stood in a basement in Calamba and prayed a prayer that could not be long enough.  The ICC investigation is a longer prayer.  It is the world saying to the dead and the detained and the mothers and the surrenderees and the seventeen-year-old in the alley: we have not forgotten you.  We are slow.  We are imperfect.  We are an institution made of paper and procedure and we cannot bring anyone back.  But we have not forgotten.

That is what love demands.  Not speed.  Not perfection.  Remembering.  The dock remembers.  The witnesses remember.  The court remembers.  The dock is not empty.  Duterte sits in it.

The hands that reached through the bars in that basement were reaching for this too.  They did not know it.  They were reaching for Jesus and for hope and for someone to tell them they were not broken beyond fix.  But they were also reaching for a world where the man who put them behind those bars would one day sit behind a different kind of bar.  Not iron.  Not in a basement.  Glass and steel and clean lines, in a building where the temperature is always correct, in a country where the crying does not have to stop because visitors came downstairs.

The dock is not empty.  Duterte is in it.  Behind bars now, the same as the men he put there.  But he is still reaching through them — not for Jesus, not for the most excellent way, but for the chair.  Still campaigning.  Still planning.  Still grasping for the hard way even from inside the system the hard way built.  Sixty men in six bunkbeds reached through bars toward something better.  Duterte reaches through bars toward the same thing that put everyone else behind them.

Nobody is broken beyond fix.  Not even him.  But he has to reach in the right direction.  And from The Hague, he is still reaching for the chair.

The hands in this book all reach.  The question is what you reach for.

The reckoning happened in glass and steel.  But there is another reckoning happening in language, and that one is closer to home.
